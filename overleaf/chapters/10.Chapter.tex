\section{Introduction to Word
Embeddings}\label{introduction-to-word-embeddings}

The history of human language is tied to the evolution of our species
Luef (2018), and it is constantly changing (Milroy 1995). Our language
encodes emotions, experiences, cultural significance, and context very
effectively. There seems to be a very tight relationship between
language and thinking, and they affect each other in several ways, such
as in the perception of time, space, or even colors Winawer et al.
(2007). Words as we hear, read, or write can be transformed into vectors
of numbers that capture information not only about the word itself but
also about its context and the link to other related words. Word
embeddings is a concept that has been part of both AI academic and
industrial discourse for quite some time. The name does not ring a bell
immediately, perhaps because it originates from mathematics: an
embedding is a function that maps elements from one space into another
(Haroske and Triebel 2011). The concept has been extended to NLP, where
words, as the main components of the human language, are mapped into a
mathematical concept represented by a continuous vector space (Tomáš
Mikolov, Yih, and Zweig 2013), becoming embeddings. Word embeddings have
been used in several disciplines for research work in finance, where
they are used to generate financial lexicons (Das et al. 2022), in
historical corpora (Wevers and Koolen 2020), in agriculture (Guo et al.
2021), or music (A. Kumar, Rajpal, and Rathore 2018), just to name a
few.

Word embeddings have seen a growing number of applications in marketing.
For example, Timoshenko and Hauser (2019) use word embeddings and a
machine learning classifier to identify customer needs from
User-Generated Content. Toubia, Berger, and Eliashberg (2021) uses
embeddings to quantify the semantic progression of a successful
(marketing) narrative. More recently, embeddings have been applied to
evaluate and to predict that performance of email campaigns and to
recommended targeting policies of new promotions, e.g. (Ellickson et al.
2024), or to extract relevant contextual product characteristics and
other information from unstructured text and image data to use this
information for demand estimation or to derive product recommendations
(Sisodia, Burnap, and Kumar 2024; Compiani, Morozov, and Seiler 2023),
just to mention a few.

Word embeddings can be trained on large text corpora and allow for fast
access to semantic relationships and similarities between, for example,
specific products and brand names (e.g., ``Kindle Fire'' and
``Amazon''), but also to their objective characteristics expressed in
words such as ``tablet,'' ``e-book reader,'' ``gift,'' or ``customer
service.'' While word embeddings focus on the process of mapping words
into a vector space, a word vector is a directly related concept and
refers to how a word is mapped into a numerical sequence.

\section{What is a Word Vector?}\label{what-is-a-word-vector}

In the following simple example, we focus on words that are related to
emotions, and we represent them through two components: polarity and
intensity. Their numerical values are encoded in a very subjective way.
Polarity, which can be negative or positive, is expressed as a range
between -1 (negative) and 1 (positive). Similarly, intensity can also
take values over a defined range, such as (0,1). When there is no
emotional quality present in a word, the value is 0, while when the
emotion is intense, the value is 1. The subjective emotional quality of
a word like ``Joy'' can be positive and intense, and it can be

two-dimensional space, as a scatterplot, and indeed in three-, four- or
any n-dimensional space, there is a correspondence between points and

dimension, one component.

The distance from the origin to a point is called magnitude, but vectors
can also provide direction, so they can capture the orientation as well.
While it is not possible to apply mathematics to words, doing so with
numerical transformations of these words is feasible, resulting in
values represented on a surface (or in a space). The similarity of these
values is equivalent to the similarity in the meaning of the words,
which can be calculated through a measure called cosine similarity.

\section{Cosine Similarity}\label{cosine-similarity-1}

Cosine similarity is a popular metric to evaluate the similarity between
two vectorized entities, such as vector representations of words or,
more generally speaking, text. This is accomplished by computing the
cosine value of the angle between them, with a value of zero (i.e.,
\(\cos(0)=1\)) indicating the vectors are identical. If the vectors are
opposite, they form a 180-degree angle, such as in \(2\pi\), and
\(cos(2\pi)=-1\), indicating that they are opposite. If the vectors are
perpendicular, the angle is 90 degrees, which can be expressed as
\(\frac{\pi}{2}\), and \(cos(\frac{\pi}{2})=0\), indicating
unrelatedness. In case you are rusty on the topic, a refresher on
trigonometry can be useful here, especially of the trigonometric circle.

To better illustrate this with an example, let's consider two words,
``Fear'' and ``Anxiety,'' with their vector representations: Fear
{[}0.25, 0.7{]} and Anxiety {[}0.25, 0.8{]}. Let us denominate the angle
between these two vectors by \(\theta\). The cosine similarity between
the vectors \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\) is given by the formula::

\[
\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{A}}{|\mathbf{F}| \times |\mathbf{A}|}
\]

The numerator represents the dot product of the vectors:
\(\textbf{F} \cdot \textbf{A} = (-0.6 \times -0.7) + (0.7 \times 0.8)\),
while the denominator is the product of the magnitudes of the vectors:
\textbar{}\textbf{F}\textbar{} \times \textbar{}\textbf{A}\textbar{} =
\(\sqrt{(-0.6)^2 + (0.7)^2} \times \sqrt{(-0.7)^2 + (0.8)^2}\).

Plugging these values into the formula, we obtain a cosine similarity
value of approximately 0.999948. This high similarity (close to 1)
reflects that these emotional expressions are perceived as very similar,
both in everyday understanding and in their numerical representation. A
cosine similarity close to 1 indicates that there is a very small angle
between these two vectors.

Now, let us try to understand how these numbers are generated. An
intuitive idea is given by the fact that in a large corpus, similar
words appear in similar contexts through their co-occurrence and
generate similar vectors, i.e., similar embeddings. Word embeddings are
used to assist or to improve tasks you have seen, such as sentiment
analysis, topic modeling, classification, or even translation, by
leveraging the semantic information of words encoded in the vectors. A
popular algorithm to establish word embeddings that try to capture
semantic similarities between words is \textbf{Word2Vec}, (Tomas Mikolov
et al. 2013).

\section{Word Embeddings Creation}\label{word-embeddings-creation}

Works of linguists such as Ferdinand de Saussure (1857 - 1913) or
anthropologists with strong contributions to linguistics, such as Franz
Boas (1858 - 1942), underlined the importance of context in
understanding language. In 1957, the British linguist John Rupert Firth,
in his distributional theory, which studies how the distribution of
words in a text can reveal their meaning, coined the phrase ``You shall
know a word by the company it keeps'' (Evert et al. 2008). The simple
intuition behind this concept is that the meaning of the words is
determined by the context they appear in, i.e., by the other words
surrounding them. Some semantic aspects of the words can be defined by
homonymy or polysemy. Homonymy refers to the case that words can look
the same but have unrelated meanings. Terms like ``mean'' can ``mean''
(pun intended) or signify unpleasant, primitive, or average. Polysemy
describes situations when words can have related meanings, like in the
sentence ``The frame was beautiful, but the painter was framed,'' the
first occurrence of the word ``frame'' refers to the frame of a
painting, while in the second one, it refers to a painter falsely
incriminated. In both homonymy and polysemy, context analysis is
necessary to disambiguate the target words.

An NLP algorithm in the spirit of Firth's theories, that focuses on
words in context and does not directly capture polysemy or homonymy is
\textbf{Word2Vec}. Similarly to the example of the contextualized
emotional vector space, it represents words as a sequence of several
numerical values in a more generic vector space. Note that the same word
in \textbf{Word2Vec}, despite a different context, cannot be represented
by different sequences of numbers. There are two main procedures to
perform \textbf{Word2Vec}: Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) and Skip-gram.
The first one predicts a target word based on the surrounding words, and
the second one tries to predict the surrounding words based on a target
word. How this is accomplished we will explain in this chapter. Before
doing so, we need you to familiarize yourself with some more basic
concepts.

The words in a corpus will be transformed into embedding numbers created
through classical training of a neural network. Like all the tasks in
artificial intelligence, the purpose of this operation is a predictive
one. Let us consider Figure~\ref{fig-w2vconcept} and understand how
\textbf{Word2Vec} is conceptually implemented.

``summer''. Then this vector is sent to the output layer which is a
classifier. The projection layer, where the embeddings matrix resides,
is used as the learned mapping that transforms the one-hot vector into a
continuous vector (embedding). The softmax function converts the output
to a probability distribution over the vocabulary. The result will be a
vector of the same dimension of the vocabulary but containing the
probabilities of which words are nearby the input word. For more
information, see McCormick (2016).

\section{Algorithms in Language Model
Training}\label{algorithms-in-language-model-training}

For training a word embedding, we first need to provide the training
algorithm with context surrounding individual words. This is
accomplished by defining the concept of sliding window. By moving the
window over the text, several context-target pairs are generated. They
capture the local context of words by building structured examples,
helping the model to learn which words appear together frequently and
identifying meaningful relationships inside the corpus. The window
represents a certain number of words before (and after) a target word
and can generate a dataset to train a model. The number of words can be
any, but some numbers capture information better than others. This
information assesses what words are near other words. Statistically
speaking, if a word appears in a specific context, other words related
to that context will be there, too. Let us start with a simple example,
with a window of two words, on the sentence we already used: ``The
summer sale campaign went viral on social media and increased brand
awareness.''

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/sliding_window_table.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-training}The sliding window concept}

\end{figure}%

In Figure~\ref{fig-training}, two tables can be seen. Each row shows the
advancement of the sliding window, which is made of two words. The word
after that is the target word. Visually, the two-word window slides to
the right, capturing just a part of the sentence. In the table to the
right, a dataset is built to save all the information captured from the
sliding window. Each pair of two words used as an input will have a
single word associated with it as an output of the dataset we generated.

To take a step further to morph the window differently, we select two
words before and two words after a target word, and obtain the basic
idea of the CBOW approach (Jang, Kim, and Kim 2019). So, given an even
number of words as input defined by a specific window size (for example
two), the output is the word in the middle. Note that in this approach,
the target word is not part of the input but it is the word that is
omitted and then predicted based on the surrounding context words.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/cbow.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-cbow}CBOW approach}

\end{figure}%

In Figure~\ref{fig-cbow}, the sliding window technique is applied with
the CBOW approach to a sentence. Each line in the table represents a
subset of the sentence, showing how the window captures different
segments as it moves from left to right. Here we try to predict a target
word based on the surrounding context words, two ahead and two after it.
The two shades of gray represent the sliding window words (input in
light gray) and the target word (output in dark gray), respectively.

A complementary approach is to have an input word and guess what the two
words before and the two words after it would be. This is demonstrated
in Figure~\ref{fig-trainingprocess} which adds further information and
shows how the training process for the word embedding model process is
performed. For example, by applying the above sliding window approach
(i.e., using one input and two candidate target words before and after
the input) the input word ``sale'' is associated with ``the,''
``summer,'' ``campaign,'' and ``went,'' and the training dataset
continues with the next input word and the related words. At the very
beginning of the process, the model contains no information given by
random embeddings, meaning that the model is untrained. Having the word
``sale,'' which word would be a neighbor? In an untrained model, the
prediction would be random, and, likely, it cannot learn any meaningful
word representations (yet); its predictions are totally inaccurate.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/training_process.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-trainingprocess}Word embedding training process}

\end{figure}%

In Figure~\ref{fig-trainingprocess}, the model prediction column shows
the probabilities the model assigns to each word in the vocabulary as
the possible neighboring word for ``sale.'' The ``Actual Target'' column
shows an example of the correct neighboring word as a so-called
``one-hot encoded'' vector (in this case, ``the''), which means to apply
a value of 1 to the correct word and a zero to all the others. The Error
column represents the difference between the actual target and the value
obtained from the untrained model. This error value is used to update
the model's parameters through backpropagation, and then retraining (Goh
1995; Rumelhart et al. 2013). Backpropagation is a fundamental method
used to train neural networks. The error value is ``sent'' backwards
through the network to adjust the weights. The operation is performed
several times, until the network learns to perform more accurate
predictions.

After we did the same process to cover all the samples in the dataset,
we concluded one epoch of training. The process is repeated for several
epochs until the model becomes stable and yields significant results or
until a certain number of epochs is planned to be performed. Eventually,
we have obtained a trained model, and we can extract the word embedding
matrix from it, which is our main goal.

\section{Optimizing Embeddings}\label{optimizing-embeddings}

In calculating the prediction of the next word, the most computationally
expensive part is the projection of the output vocabulary. To improve
this task, first, we need to generate high-quality embeddings; then, we
need to use these embeddings to train a language model (for the
next-word prediction). At this point, we assume that we have a set of
fully trained embeddings and we can switch now from the embedding
training phase to applying them in a predictive task. Now, instead of
predicting diretcly a neighboring word, the model calculates a score for
a given input-output word pair to indicate if they are neighbors or not
(1 or 0). This switch effectively changes the model from a regular
neural network for next-word prediction into a logistic regression
model, which is simpler and faster to compute. The structure of our
dataset slightly changes by adding a new column assessing the presence
of two neighboring words. Now, we also do not look to guess the next
word anymore, but if the words are neighbors or not. This operation can
be done very fast but with one caveat. If all the examples are positive,
meaning that each target value is 1, we have a model that always returns
1, meaning a perfect accuracy, which leads to a clear overfitting case.
To overcome this, negative samples of words that are not neighbors are
randomly sampled from the vocabulary, and the target value 0 is assigned
to those word pairs. This concept is introduced by Gutmann and Hyvärinen
(2010) and represented in Figure~\ref{fig-neg}.

\centering{

Before the training process starts, we must check which words are
involved and how many words the vocabulary of the corpus contains. Let
us combine skipgram and negative sampling methodologies and show how
\textbf{Word2Vec} uses them in the training process. At the start of the
training phase, the embedding and context matrices are created, see
Figure~\ref{fig-embeddingcontext}. It can be noticed that they have
exactly the same size, containing the same vocabulary and the same
number of features. What is distinct between them, is that the values of
the features will be different, and after the conclusion of the training
process, there will be two distinct matrices, but same sized.

\caption{\label{fig-embeddingcontext}Embedding and context matrices}

\end{figure}%

As in any training process, the matrices are initially populated with
random values. Both hold unique embedding sequences for each word in the
vocabulary, meaning that a word in the embedding matrix will have
different values in the context matrix. Each word is represented as a
row. The second dimension of the matrices holds the number of features,
a concept similar to the emotional space where we had just two: valence
and intensity. While 300 is a commonly employed value, the number of
features can range between 50 and 500 or any number you find suitable.
It is important to note that they do not have any predetermined label
assigned to them. Figure~\ref{fig-finaltwo} shows how the algorithm
updates model parameters. A series of computational steps are performed
to improve the word embeddings, i.e., the correspondence between words
and context.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/finaltwo.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-finaltwo}Word2vec training process}

\end{figure}%

The training mechanism of the two matrices, embedding and context,
follows a standard procedure of training a neural network using
backpropagation, mentioned in the previous section. There is a forward
pass, a loss or error function calculation, a backward pass (called
backpropagation), and a parameter update. At the beginning, in the first
forward pass, the values in the matrices, also called weights or
parameters, are populated with randomly assigned values to ensure
effective learning. If we consider Figure~\ref{fig-finaltwo} again, the
dot product of input and output \(\text{input}\cdot\text{output}\) is
calculated using random values. The result is passed on to the sigmoid()
function and yields a value between 0 and 1, which, in turn, is compared
against the value in the \textbf{target} column to compute the error.
Hence, the loss function part is executed here. This is an extremely
important value because, based on this, the model parameters are
iteratively updated to make the error value as close as possible to 0.
The updating procedure is done through backpropagation {[}reference{]}
from the output layer to the input, updating the parameters through an
optimization algorithm called gradient descent.

\subsubsection{Training process details}\label{training-process-details}

Training a neural network, such as this case, involves several potential
optimizations done on the hyperparameters. This term is a combination of
two words: ``hyper,'' from Greek, meaning ``above'' or ``beyond,'' and
parameter, which initially was composed of two words: ``para,'' meaning
``beside,'' and ``metron,'' meaning ``measure.'' So, a hyperparameter is
a variable that operates before training begins at a higher level, which
can influence the final model. One such parameter is the window size,
which can have a strong impact on the outcome. A small window learns
specific and tight relationships between words, such as ``milk'' and
``shake''. A larger window learns broader relationships such as ``milk''
and ``animal''. Another parameter is the dimensionality of the word
vector (the columns in Figure~\ref{fig-rect1}). A higher dimension
captures subtle relationships between words, but it can lead to
overfitting and increased computational costs. A lower dimension is
faster but might not capture all the nuances of the relationships
between words.

What is being calculated at this point is the gradient of the error with
respect to the embedding vectors needs to be calculated. This involves
computing the partial derivatives of the loss function with respect to
each element of the embedding and context vectors. This step is
essential to update the parameters, both for embedding and context
matrices. The generic update formula can be expressed as:

\[
\theta = \theta - \eta \cdot \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta)
\] where \(\theta\) is the parameter being updated, \(\eta\) is the
learning rate, \(\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta)\) is the gradient of the cost
function \(J(\theta)\) with respect to the parameters \(\theta\).

Both the embedding and context matrices follow the same steps. Each
word's vector is updated by slowly moving in the direction that reduces
the error. This involves subtracting a constant fraction, identified by
\(\eta\), of the gradient of the loss with respect to the embedding
vector from the current vector.

Let us denote the embedding vector of the input word as \(v\) and the
context vector of the output word as \(u\), and the error calculated as
E, the updates can be mathematically expressed as:

\[
v = v - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial E}{\partial v}
\]

\[
u = u - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial E}{\partial u}
\]

These equations represent gradient descent updates for the parameters
\(v\) and \(u\) respectively, where \(\eta\) is the learning rate, and
\(\frac{\partial E}{\partial v}\) and \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial u}\)
are the gradients of the error function E with respect to \(v\) and
\(u\).

The partial derivatives of the error function E with respect to the
variables \(v\) and \(u\), \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial v}\) and
\(\frac{\partial E}{\partial u}\), depend on the specific form of the
error function used. It often involves the sigmoid function of the dot
product of \(v\) and \(u\), compared against the actual target (1 or 0
in our case). This process is repeated for each training example,
gradually modifying the values of the embeddings by minimizing the
overall error in predicting the context words given the input words. The
loss function J and the Error are closely related, and in many cases,
they are essentially the same thing. The loss function (J) is typically
defined for an entire dataset. It represents the overall performance of
the model across multiple examples, while the error (E) usually refers
to the error for a single training example. In the context of Word2Vec
and similar models, we often use the terms interchangeably for a single
example. The loss function J for the entire dataset would be the sum (or
average) of the individual errors E for each training example.

Let us focus on a practical and simple example to understand exactly
what happens. Considering how the procedure was described in
Figure~\ref{fig-finaltwo} we can define our error function as:

\[
E = t - \sigma(v \cdot u)
\] where \(\sigma()\) is the sigmoid function, and \(t\) is the target
(0 or 1).

Calculate the partial derivatives, knowing that the derivative of the
sigmoid function is \(\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\left(1 - \sigma(x)\right)\)
and the derivative of a constant value, such as t, results in \(t'=0\).

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial v} = -\sigma(v \cdot u)(1 - \sigma(v \cdot u)) \cdot u
\]

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial u} = -\sigma(v \cdot u)(1 - \sigma(v \cdot u)) \cdot v
\]

Update the vectors: \[
v_{\text{new}} = v_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot \left(-\sigma(v_{\text{old}} \cdot u_{\text{old}})(1 - \sigma(v_{\text{old}} \cdot u_{\text{old}})) \cdot u_{\text{old}}\right)
\]

\[
u_{\text{new}} = u_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot \left(-\sigma(v_{\text{old}} \cdot u_{\text{old}})(1 - \sigma(v_{\text{old}} \cdot u_{\text{old}})) \cdot v_{\text{old}}\right)
\]

We can use a numerical example to better understand the whole process.

\[
v = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5, 0.3 \end{bmatrix}
\] \[
u = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1, 0.2 \end{bmatrix}
\] \[
t = 1 \quad \text{(positive example)}
\] \[
\eta = 0.1 \quad \text{(learning rate)}
\]

Calculate: \[
v \cdot u = 0.5 \cdot 0.1 + 0.3 \cdot 0.2 = 0.11
\] \[
\sigma(0.11) \approx 0.5275
\] Error: \[
E = 1 - 0.5275 = 0.4725
\]

Gradient for (v): \[
-\sigma(0.11) \cdot (1 - \sigma(0.11)) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0.1, 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.0249, -0.0498 \end{bmatrix}
\]

Update (v): \[
v_{\text{new}} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.5, 0.3 \end{bmatrix} - 0.1 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} -0.0249, -0.0498 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.50249, 0.30498 \end{bmatrix}
\] One can notice that the new values of the vector are close to the
original ones, but by performing this task several times, the values
will be adjusted to obtain an optimal Error function.

\section{Other Embeddings and Models}\label{other-embeddings-and-models}

There are other popular algorithms to represent the vector space, such
as Global Vectors (GloVe) (Pennington, Socher, and Manning 2014),
developed at Stanford, and FastText (Bojanowski et al. 2017), developed
by Facebook (now Meta). The aim is, as in the Word2Vec case, to capture
meaning in the text following an unsupervised algorithm. GloVe is based
on the co-occurrence matrix concept, which is built for an entire
corpus, summarizing how often any word pairs occur together. The main
idea is that ratios of co-occurrence probabilities contain useful
information. Words like ``coffee'' and ``cappuccino'' would have similar
vector representations, as they frequently appear (co-occur) together in
determined contexts: social meetings, breakfasts, bars, etc. While
Word2Vec focuses on context through sliding windows, GloVe uses a
co-occurrence matrix for the entire corpus. FastText, instead, is a
further development that builds on Word2Vec. It incorporates subword
information to improve their vector representation, meaning that words
are broken down into character n-grams. This division improves the
possibility of including underrepresented words that are
out-of-vocabulary. Such examples can be words that do not appear in the
training session but appear in a corpus to be analyzed. So, if a new
word appears, it is broken down into familiar subwords (or n-grams).
FastText performs well to capture how morphological details are defined.
Languages with many words can benefit the most from this algorithm. For
example, German is a great fit because it has many compound words and
inflectional changes, and it is morphologically rich.

There is an increasing number of other methods that are designed to
learn word embeddings. For example, Doc2Vec (Le and Mikolov 2014)
associates a unique vector with each document or paragraph, focusing on
larger portions of text rather than single words. Sent2Vec (Pagliardini,
Gupta, and Jaggi 2017) builds sentence-level representations from
unigrams and bigrams, Embeddings from Language Models (ELMo; Peters et
al. (2018)) focuses on a sentence context to generate word vectors,
(Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) (BERT; Devlin
(2018)) uses the transformer architecture to generate context-aware
embeddings, or GPT (Radford 2018), another transformer-based
architecture, that became extremely popular starting with the GPT-3
version in 2020 (Brown 2020). Transformers represent a further
development of the word vectors as they allow for capturing context or
generating text that fits nicely into a specific context. We briefly
discuss the building blocks of this architecture next.

\subsection{Transformers}\label{transformers}

A transformer is an important neural network type. It uses word
embeddings to learn context and implicitly meaning by detecting
relationships between words in sentences. Transformers are the main
architectural frame in unsupervised learning models underlying BERT or
GPT. The link to word embeddings in the previous section is that the
input tokens, which are words or subwords, are transformed into arrays
of numbers, but with a twist. Unlike in Word2Vec, transformers add a
position encoding to the vectors to ensure that the model captures the
position of each word in a sentence. This dual information is passed on
to the actual transformer model. Transformers rely on the self-attention
mechanism (introduced by Vaswani (2017)) to process input sequences in
parallel. The transformer architecture includes an encoder (which
processes the input sequence) and sometimes, but not always, a decoder
(which generates the output sequence). In addition, there are multi-head
self-attention layers, which run in parallel and focus on different
aspects of the input. After the attention layer, feedforward layers are
applied, and positional encodings are used. Three types of transformers
can be identified: (i) encoder-only, which are designed to understand
text (e.g., BERT); (ii) decoder-only, which are designed to generate
text (e.g., GPT); and (iii) encoder-decoder sequences, which are useful
for language translation. In this subchapter, the basic explanations on
transformers were inspired from Alammar and Grootendorst (2024).

\subsubsection{Encoder-decoder}\label{encoder-decoder}

To illustrate and to better understand the transformer concept,
Figure~\ref{fig-transformer} presents a classic encoder-decoder
architecture. The diagram shows how language translation can be
processed. The phrase ``to be happy'' is sent to the encoder made of six
layers, each one representing a processing state. The output from the
decoder leads to the correct output translation in German, ``glücklich
sein.''

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/transformer.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-transformer}Transformer, encoder and decoder}

\end{figure}%

A more detailed representation of the encoder and decoder layers sheds
more light into their internals (see
Figure~\ref{fig-encoderanddecoder}). The encoder consists of a Feed
Forward Neural Network that processes data through a series of
transformations and a Self-Attention mechanism to evaluate the
importance of different sections of the input, aiming at capturing
dependencies and relationships in the phrase. The Decoder has its own
Feed Forward Neural Network, which is used to produce the output, an
Encoder-Decoder Attention part, which allows the decoder to focus on the
important parts of the input sequence, and the Self-Attention mechanism
to consider the previous output when generating the next token.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/encoder_decoder.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-encoderanddecoder}Transformer: encoder and decoder
detail}

\end{figure}%

To illustrate, let us focus now on the encoder part and add more detail
by zooming in into one layer. Let us have the initially vectorized
sequence (such as in Word2Vec) for the words ``To'', ``be'', and
``happy'' denoted each as \(x_1, x_2,x_3\). When applying of the
self-attention mechanism, we obtain another embedding, which will
capture the context. For example, ``to'' is influenced by ``be'' and
``happy'', the relative importance of the words, the position (not shown
in this image, allowing to consider the order of words), some features
of the words that may be emphasized, or the semantic relationships
between words. The new embedding, given by \(z_1, z_2, z_3\), is a more
refined and context-aware embedding representation. The transformation
from \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) into \(r_1, r_2, r_3\) within the encoder layers,
is achieved by applying a point-wise feed-forward network to further
process the attention output and to extract and refine features from the
z vectors (for more detailed information, cf. Vaswani (2017)). Including
the many layers is important so that at each of them can refine the
vector representation for precision.

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=\textheight]{figure/chapter10/encoder.png}

}

\caption{A detailed perspective on Encoder}

\end{figure}%

After processing through all the layers, the final output of the encoder
stack will contain a context-aware representation of the input sequence
and serves as the bridge between the Encoder and Decoder. The main idea
is that while a word can be translated accurately without such
context-enriched information, doing the same for a phrase would get
immensely more precision if the context is included. At the end of the
encoder for the phrase ``to be happy,'' a set of contextualized
representations for each word in the sentence is produced. While as an
input we have ``to be happy'' (3 tokens), the output contains three
vectors, one for each word, typically of dimension \(d_{model}=512\) or
\(d_{model}=768\) as in many implementations.

Specifically, the vector for ``to'' becomes:
\(\vec{v}_{to} = [v_{to,1}, v_{to,2}, \ldots, v_{to,d_{model}}]\), the
vector for ``be'' becomes:
\(\vec{v}_{be} = [ v_{be,1}, v_{be,2}, \ldots, v_{be,d_{model}}]\), and
the vector for ``happy'' becomes:
\(\vec{v}_{happy} = [ v_{happy,1}, v_{happy,2}, \ldots, v_{happy,d_{model}}]\).

In other words, each of the vectors
\(\vec{v}_{to}, \vec{v}_{be}, \vec{v}_{happy}\) contains information not
only about the word itself, but also about its context inside the
sentence. In this layered approach, the self-attention mechanism yields
a result that captures the interactions among words, and feed-forward
layers in the encoder refine these contextual representations. The
numerical representation expressed as a vector for the word ``to''
contains information about its relationship to ``be'' and ``happy,'' the
one for ``be'' contains information about its relationship to ``to'' and
``happy'', and the one for ``happy'' contains information about its
relationship to ``to'' and ``be.'' These final output vectors capture
semantic and syntactic information about each word in the context of the
whole sentence. Transformers can be used to accomplish several tasks,
such as classification, translation, or even language generation. But
they have also potential for being employed for other (predictive or
generative) tasks involving sequential information other than language,
such as histories of customer-firm interactions (e.g., Kim, McCarthy,
and Lee (2025)) or market basket data (Gabel and Ringel 2024).

In the next section, we will focus on the more basic Word2Vec
application, while in the successive one, we will follow a transformers
approach.

adding ``bad'', would lean towards terms that are not favorable. We
could write it like ``If (the word) kindle is associated to good, what
words associate kindle to bad?''. This example demonstrates how we can
arithmetically process word vectors, the outcome shows how useful this
operation can be.

In this subchapter, we will illustrate the use of transformers via the R

example topic modeling, named entity recognition, sentiment analysis,
and many more. These themes have already been seen in the previous

\subsection{Text Summarization}\label{text-summarization}

Summarization is important in marketing (Hartmann and Netzer 2023)
because it helps the marketing analyst to condense large volumes of
customer feedback into shorter, concise, actionable insights. The
operation is useful to understand customer sentiment, or to identify
trends at scale. In the next coding example we summarize a series of
randomly selected customer reviews. We set a seed for reproducibility,
we filter the dataset for the Amazon brand, and we randomly select 100
reviews, to speed up the computational process. These selected reviews
are then concatenated into a single string, to produce a single
document. Then, we apply the \texttt{textSum()} function and by using a
pre-trained deep learning model (t5-small) we summarize the text. The
summary in output is generated as a string that will be between 5 and
100 tokens long. The summarized result is stored in the \texttt{ts}
variable which will be printed using the \texttt{cat()} function.

\subsection{Information Search, Question and Answering Using
Transformers
(Q\&A)}\label{information-search-question-and-answering-using-transformers-qa}

Information search and customer feedback play a central role in modern
marketing. Transformers revolutionized information search by enabling
accurate, context-aware retrieval, allowing brands to analyze vast
amounts of consumer queries and intent with high precision. Also
transformer-powered chatbots enhance customer interactions and
automating question and answering for more personalized engagement
(Smith and Albaum 2010). Q\&As represent direct insights into customer
experiences, pain points, or experiences. In contrast to surveys Q\&As
provide real-time feedback, allowing to discover unexpected insights, a
more conversational approach in these dynamic exchanges, and
scalability. Q\&As will not substitute surveys, especially when there is
the need for structured information on aspects of a product with the
corresponding statistical analysis. Nevertheless, there is a growing
body of literature that explores the capabilities of transformer-based
language models to mimic consumer research (for an overview, see N.
Arora, Chakraborty, and Nishimura (2025); Sarstedt et al. (2024)).
Despite the enthusiasm for ``silicon sampling'' there is recent research
that also identified its limitations (e.g., Goli and Singh (2024);
Stromberg et al. (2025)).

As an ending note, the models presented in this chapter, find text or
generate text by repeating (like a parrot) patterns found in the
training data, but truly understanding or reasoning about language is
still a human quality. Machines can produce output based on statistical
probabilities (the word stochastic is used in these cases), but would
not (yet) have a comprehension of the meaning, as stated in Bender et
al. (2021).

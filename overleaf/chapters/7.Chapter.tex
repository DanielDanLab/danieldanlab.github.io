\section{Identifying Latent Themes in Text
Data}\label{identifying-latent-themes-in-text-data}

According to the Oxford Dictionary, a topic is ``a subject that you
talk, write, or learn about.'' In simpler terms, it can be understood as
a theme. For instance, a newspaper may cover several topics such as
politics, crime, lifestyle, economy, and sports. Each of these topics is
characterized by (combinations of) specific words, a fact that readers
intuitively recognize. Regarding our tablet dataset and the marketing
perspective, topics can represent certain product characteristics, brand
comparisons, evaluative aspects and sentiments about products and
services (such as, value for the money, product quality, or customer
service), to name a few.

But what exactly constitutes a model? Depending on the field of
expertise, it can refer to many things, but modern marketing is linked
with a lot of other disciplines such as data science and statistics. The
latter is related to data-driven models such as linear regression, which
summarize or describe datasets, ultimately aiming to predict new data
points. Statistical models try to explain an idealized and simplified
reality through mathematical representations (Micceri 1989). In
statistics, mathematical formulas can be represented with beautiful
graphs and curves that are extremely precise, but real data are
approximations of those shapes. Complex realities are thus transformed
into idealized, simplified mathematical representations. Perfect
straight lines or circles will never perfectly describe real data; these
shapes do not exist in nature. However, the closer the representations
are to a mathematical model, the better we can compare different
datasets. We do this because we need benchmarking terms for comparison.
Comparison is not just a theoretical exercise but a practical tool for
decision-making, enabling marketers to evaluate strategies, forecast
trends, and understand customer behaviors more effectively.

The generic and pompous definition for topic modeling, is that it is an
unsupervised statistical model used in text analytics, that clusters
words into topics without predefined classes, revealing latent themes
within large text corpora. This technique is essential for analyzing
customer reviews or social media discourse, enabling the detection of
trends, sentiments, and emerging issues without manually reviewing each
single document. Typical applications in marketing comprise the analysis
of social media postings or online product reviews (e.g., Tirunillai and
Tellis (2014), Büschken and Allenby (2016)), but also purchase records
(e.g., Jacobs, Donkers, and Fok (2016)) of online browsing behavior
(e.g., Schröder et al. (2019); Trusov, Ma, and Jamal (2016)). For an
extensive overview of topic modeling applications in marketing see
Reisenbichler and Reutterer (2019). From a practical perspective, topic
modeling can inform marketing strategies by identifying what aspects
customers focus on, such as product features or service quality. For
instance, in a dataset of online hotel reviews, topics like
`Cleanliness' or `Service' can emerge and be further analyzed for
sentiment, frequency, and trend over time, providing actionable insights
for addressing customer concerns. Determining the optimal number of
topics (K) is a critical step in topic modeling. It is often approached
through educated guesses informed by domain knowledge or systematic
methods like coherence scores. Choosing the right (K), alongside the
interpretation of the words that characterize topics, lead to a blend
between art and science.

Some commonly used topic modelling techniques used in marketing are
Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) (D. M. Blei, Ng, and Jordan 2003),
Structured Topic Modeling (STM) (Roberts, Stewart, and Airoldi 2016),
Correlated Topic Modeling (CTM) (D. Blei and Lafferty 2006),
Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) (Lee and Seung 2000), Latent
Semantic Analysis (LSA)(Landauer, Foltz, and Laham 1998), or Dynamic
Topic Models (DTM) (D. M. Blei and Lafferty 2006). For a more complete
list, check (Jelodar et al. 2019), but in this chapter we will focus on
topic modeling with LDA and STM.

\section{Latent Dirichlet Allocation}\label{latent-dirichlet-allocation}

One of the most studied topic modelling algorithms is LDA. The algorithm
aggregates words into topics based on their distribution across
documents, uncovering the `hidden' topics that characterize the corpus.
In practice, we have a set of documents that contains topics
characterized by specific words. The topic is something more at a
conceptual level, like a \# (hashtag) that describes the content of a
tweet message or an Instagram image. The intuition behind LDA as a
generative model is that writing a document or a review of a product is
driven by some themes one wishes to share and adds this to that
document. In our dataset containing tablets, one would think of the
characteristics of such a device: for example the screen. Is it bright?
Is it responsive? What about the connectivity? Is it fast? 5G, bluetooth
or WiFi? Connectivity and screen are themes or topics. These topics are
described by specific words, but also by words that are shared between
multiple topics. In a review, as well as in any written document, some
themes will be more prevalent than others. If the customer service fell
short of the client's expectations, the theme will emerge. And the words
that characterize it would be more prevalent for this one than for other
topics. Let us consider a review that talks about \textbf{connectivity},
(in \(15\%\) of the words contained in the document), about
\textbf{screen}, (\(25\%\)), \textbf{customer service} (\(50\%\)), and
\textbf{value for the money} (\(10\%\)). To represent this as a
probability distribution over the review, it would look like a vector
{[}0.15, 0.25, 0.50, 0.1{]}. Note that their sum of the topics must be
\(1\).

Each review will have a unique distribution of topics, but this is not
all. Each topic is characterized by words, for example, the customer
service topic would contain words with their proportions like: bad
(\(0.03\%\)), back (\(0.02\%\)), never (\(0,015\%\)), and so on. Each of
these words in the vocabulary, i.e.~the whole words used in the tablet
reviews corpus, would appear distributed with a certain relative
frequency over that specific topic {[}\ldots, 0.02, \ldots, 0.03,
\ldots, 0.015, \ldots{]}. This expected proportion is the same, within
the scope of that topic, no matter which review you consider from the
corpus. So, there are two distributions: a mixture of topics over
documents, and a mixture words over topics. Because all these are
probabilities, we have a probabilistic model here. The previously
described single vectors are characteristic for realizations of a
multinomial distribution, but the concept of a conjugate prior
distribution over multinomial distributions is characteristic for the
Dirichlet distribution, hence the name of the algorithm.

To summarize, the LDA model is based on two essential assumptions: \(K\)
hidden topics are distributed across the entire collection of reviews,
and each review can be represented as a probabilistic distribution of
these topics. Each topic is, in turn, a probabilistic distribution of
words from the dataset's vocabulary.

Let us demystify this algorithm and understand its functioning. As
reference of how LDA is implemented, check Figure~\ref{fig-lda} (Image
produced by the authors, inspired from (D. M. Blei, Ng, and Jordan
2003).

Let us give the definitions of the terms that appear in the image, then
detail how the algorithm works.

\(M\) is the fixed total number of documents. In a specific corpus
\(D\), documents will be indexed by \(i \in \{1,...,M\}\)

\(N\) is the number of words in a given document \(i\). \(N_i\)
represents how many words document \(i\) holds, i.e.~the length of the
document.

\(\alpha\) is the (hyper)parameter of the Dirichlet prior on the
per-document topic distributions. This describes, how topics are
distributed in each document.

\(\beta\) beta is the (hyper)parameter of the Dirichlet prior on the
per-topic word distribution. This describes how words are distributed
inside each topic.

\(\theta_i\) the topic distribution for document \(i\), modeled as a
random variable characterized by a Dirichlet distribution
\(\theta_i \sim \mathcal{D}(\alpha)\).

\(\phi_k\) is the word distribution for topic \(k\), where
\(k \in \{1,...,K\}\), modeled as a random variable characterized by a
Dirichlet distribution \(\phi_k \sim \mathcal{D}(\beta)\)

\(z_{ij}\) is the topic for the \(j^{th}\) word in \(i^{th}\) document.
The topic \(z_{i,j} \sim Multinomial(\theta_{i})\).

\(w_{ij}\) is a specific word, \(j\), in document \(i\). The words
\(w_{i,j} \sim Multinomial(\phi_{z_{i,j}})\)

Known as the categorical distribution, here we deal with a multinomial
distribution with one trial.

The algorithm is characterized by a two-step process. The first is
called the Generative Model (GM) which describes how documents are
created from a set of latent topics. The second step is called Inference
or Estimation Procedure (EP) and involves estimating the parameters of
the GM, for example the topic distributions for each document and the
word distributions for each topic from the documents provided. It is
that we ``learn'' the hidden topic structure.

The first step describes how LDA assumes the data are generated and
consists of 5 layers. At (I), a Dirichlet distribution characterized by
parameter \(\alpha\) is used to create the distribution of topics over
each document. If \(\alpha <1\), each document will tend to contain a
few dominant topics (i.e., the distribution is sparse). If
\(\alpha > 1\), the document will tend to contain topics distributed
more evenly (Hagg et al. 2022). At (II), parameter \(\theta\) of a
multinomial distribution, determines the shape of the distribution of
topics over each document. In other words, \(\alpha\) shapes the
multinomial \(\theta\), so each \(\theta_d\) will be different from
document to document, as shown at step (III). At (IV) we have another
Dirichlet distribution with parameter \(\beta\), this time for all the
words in the vocabulary. If \(\beta<1\), a topic will tend to be
dominated by a smaller set of characteristic words. Starting with the
first word of the first document, to the last word in the last document,
the algorithm will randomly conceptually ``assign'' each word to a
topic, based on \(\theta_d\). (V). The topic's word distribution
\(\phi_k\) is shared across documents, but be aware that each document's
topic mixture \(\theta_d\) is different. Because each topic \(k\) has a
separate \(\phi_k\), it defines its own ``theme'' characterized by
specific words.

The second step (EP) uses methods such as Gibbs sampling (our focus
here) or Variational Inference (VI) to estimate \(\theta_d\) for each
document and \(\phi_k\) for each topic from the corpus provided. We do
not know the true value of these parameters, and we can only observe the
words in each document, but we aim to infer the latent topic assignments
and distributions. In Gibbs sampling, each word's topic assignment is
re-sampled based on the current assignment of all the other words, until
a stable words over topics posterior distribution is reached. Over many
repetitions, the estimates for the \(\phi_k\) and the \(\theta_d\)
converge to a stable state. In this context, ``converge'', means that
the model parameters (\(\phi_k\) and \(\theta_d\)) reach a point where
their distribution stops changing significantly. When Gibbs converges,
we have an estimate for each document mixes topics \(\theta_d\) and how
words are distributed in each topic \(\phi_k\).

It is important to use a practical example to understand the underlying
mechanism of the Gibbs sampling, then we will provide the mathematical
formulas. Let us have a very simplified corpus made of 4 documents that
resemble the dataset in this book:

\(Doc_1\): This tablet has a bright screen.\\
\(Doc_2\): The RAM memory of the tablet is sufficient.\\
\(Doc_3\): The tablet connects to WiFi.\\
\(Doc_4\): The tablet connects to WiFi and has a superb screen.

To further simplify, we assume to have only two topics, \(T_1\) and
\(T_2\) that could represent two themes, ``hardware'' for \(T_1\) and
``connectivity'' for \(T_2\). LDA does not assign labels to topics,
assigns only generic names such as \(Topic_1\), \(Topic_2\), and so on.
We have to figure out a topic name based on the words assigned. Whenever
we apply LDA, we must preprocess the dataset, like we did in the
previous chapters with the bag-of-words approach, such as punctuation
removal, stop words removal, or lowercase. This way, our preprocessed
vocabulary, in alphabetic order, becomes: \{bright, connects, screen,
sufficient, superb, memory, tablet, wifi\}. Initially, LDA
\textbf{randomly} assigns the words to one of the two topics.

\(Doc_1\): tablet(\(T_1\)), bright (\(T_1\)), screen(\(T_2\))\\
\(Doc_2\): tablet(\(T_2\)), memory(\(T_1\)), sufficient(\(T_2\))\\
\(Doc_3\): tablet(\(T_1\)), connect(\(T_2\)), wifi(\(T_1\))\\
\(Doc_4\): tablet(\(T_2\)), connect(\(T_1\)), wifi(\(T_2\)),
superb(\(T_1\)), screen(\(T_2\))

We need to count the words in each topic.

\(T_1\): tablet (2) + bright (1) + memory (1) + connect (1) + wifi (1) +
superb (1) = 7 words\\
\(T_2\): tablet (2) + screen (2) + sufficient (1) + connect (1) + wifi
(1) = 7 words

The vocabulary size \(V\) is made of 8 terms: tablet, bright, screen,
memory, sufficient, connects, wifi, superb.

Now we are in the second step of the algorithm. After the initial random
assignment done in the first step, Gibbs sampling is used to reassign
the words to the existing topics until we obtain convergence, i.e.~until
the algorithm does not reassign these words to topics anymore. Let us
update the topic assignment for the term ``screen'' initially assigned
to Document 1. We must (temporarily) remove one instance of ``screen''
occurrence from its current assignment \(T_2\), adjust the counts, and
calculate the conditional probability for each topic. The adjusted count
for \(T_1\) is unchanged, it holds 7 terms while the adjusted count for
\(T_2\) holds 6 terms, you will see a (\(-1\)) in the formula. It is as
if this temporarily removed a term which needs to be freshly assigned to
a topic.

To calculate the conditional probabilities of topic \(k\) given word
\(w\) in document \(d\) we use the formula: Add here a citation

\[
P(T_k|w_d) \propto \left(\frac{n_{k,w}+\beta}{\sum_{w'}(n_{k,w'}+V\beta)} \right) \times \left(\frac{n_{d,k}+\alpha}{\sum_{k'}(n_{d,k'}+K\alpha)}\right) 
\] where

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(n_{k,w}\) is the count of word \(w\) assigned to topic \(k\),
  excluding current instance.
\item
  \(n_{d,k}\) is the count of all words in document \(d\) assigned to
  topic \(k\), excluding current instance.
\item
  \(\sum_{w'}(n_{k,w'}+\beta)\) is the total count of all words assigned
  to topic \(k\) plus \(\beta\) for each word in the vocabulary.
\item
  \(\sum_{k'}(n_{d,k'}+\alpha)\) is the total count of all words in
  document \(d\) plus \(\alpha\) for each topic.
\item
  \(V\) is the vocabulary size.\\
\item
  \(K\) is the number of topics.
\end{itemize}

Be aware that this formula may appear in other books or scientific
articles under similar shapes, each of them trying to obtain similar
outcomes.

Assuming hyperparameters \(\alpha = 0.1\) (for document-topic
distributions) and add reference here \(\beta = 0.01\) (for topic-term distributions) we
calculate the probability of the term ``screen'' to see if it belongs to
\(T_1\) or to \(T_2\).

\(P(T_1 |\text{screen})\) is proportional to:

Term-Topic part:
\[\frac{(n_{T_1,\text{screen}} +\beta)}{\text{Total words in} T_1 + V\times \beta} = \frac{0+0.01}{7+8\times 0.01} \]

Document-Topic part:
\[\frac{(n_{Doc_1,T_1} +\alpha)}{\text{Total words in }Doc_1 + 2 \times \alpha} = \frac{2+0.1}{3+2\times 0.1} \]

Combined probability for \(T_1\) proportional to:
\(\frac{0.01}{7.08}\times\frac{2.1}{3.2}=0.001608\)

\(P(T_2 |\text{screen})\) is proportional to:

Term-Topic part:
\[\frac{(n_{T_2, \text{screen}} +\beta -1)}{\text{Total words in } T_2 + V\times \beta -1} = \frac{2+0.01-1}{7+8\times 0.01-1} \]

Document-Topic part:
\[\frac{(n_{Doc_1,T_2}, +\alpha)}{\text{Total words in }Doc_1 + 2 \times \alpha} = \frac{1+0.1}{3+2\times 0.1} \]

Combined probability for \(T_2\) proportional to:
\(\frac{1.01}{6.08}\times\frac{1.1}{3.2}=0.0571\)

After the calculations, we need to compare the results and check where
``screen'' is more likely to belong. We notice that ``screen'' has a
higher chance of being assigned to \(T_2\), because
\(0.0571 > 0.001608\) but the final outcome depends on the many
iterations of assigning and reassigning of the word to a topic. This
procedure is done for each word in each document. In the end, what
changes from the initial random assignment of word to topics, is the
distribution of words depicted in layer (IV). Those shapes you see in
Figure~\ref{fig-lda} will change. This example simplified many steps,
but it shows the core process of LDA using Gibbs sampling.

\subsection{Seeded LDA.}\label{seeded-lda.}

Let us assume that you as an analyst already know the topics (e.g., from
a previous study on a different, but related text corpus), but you want
LDA to group the words in the topics with increased precision. Seeded
LDA allows for semi-supervised topic modeling acknowledging that you
might have prior knowledge of your dataset and you can provide a
dictionary that links already labeled topics to corresponding words.
These ``seeded'' words provide a starting point for the algorithm,
meaning that the random assignment, as it is done in the first step when
classic LDA is performed, becomes ``informed'' by the dictionary. In
order to perform this we need a dictionary function and an \texttt{yml}
file to store the information. Each line defines a topic lable such as
``hardware'' or ``functionality'', and associates it with a list of
``seed words'' which are strongly related to that topic. The structure
of the file, \texttt{dictionary.yml} looks like this.

hardware: {[}ram, gb, storage, slot, external, camera, power, wifi,
battery, button{]}

functionality: {[}video*, games, *books, email, social, media, reader,
browser{]}

customer\_service: {[}service, support, customer, return{]}

names: {[}amazon, apple, ipad, xoom, samsung, galaxy, google{]}

experience: {[}screen*, display, resolution, glass*, touch, thin,
light{]}

where the * represents a placeholder that captures plurals or other
inflectional forms. For example video* could match video, videos, and
*books can capture works like books, songbooks or notebooks.

When we employ Seeded LDA, the algorithm uses the seeded words to
initialize (guide) the topic assignments so that the model places them
(and similar words) in the corresponding topic. With respect to the
classical LDA, the random assignment step is replaced with an
``informed'' assignment based on domain knowledge.

\subsection{Apply Sequential LDA sentence based, with seeded
words}\label{apply-sequential-lda-sentence-based-with-seeded-words}

Sentence-based LDA (see, e.g. Büschken and Allenby (2016)) is an
interesting way to tokenize a corpus into documents as sentences. So
each sentence in a review represents a document. Several studies pointed
out that when people leave a review, topics are expressed in a
sentence-based manner, rarely two or more topics in a single sentence.
The technicalities to accomplish this task are solved through the
\texttt{corpus\_reshape()} function. In this case we will consider the
corpus as a merged list of sentences. Let us apply sequential LDA and
check the results. The result of the Document-Feature Matrix (DFM),
which is the equivalent of the Document-Term Matrix, will show as many
documents as the number of sentences (documents) and the sparsity of
such a matrix is very high, use \texttt{print(dfmt)} to print it. The
sequential LDA algorithm incorporates the topics discovered for the
previous sentence when modeling the next one, effectively acknowledging
that neighboring sentences in a review often share related subjects. To
clarify this step, the previous sentence does not force a topic into th
current one, but influences the model's estimate. In this case, each
sentence's topic distribution is ``guided'' but not determined by what
was found in the preceding one, because it has this bias for continuity.

allows to space between specificity and generality of the terms. It
gives the ratio of a terms' probability within a topic to its marginal
probability across the corpus. The visual representation allows for a
quick exploration of which terms are combined into what topics.

\section{Structural Topic Models}\label{structural-topic-models}

A Structural Topic Model (STM; Roberts et al. (2013), Roberts et al.
(2014)) was created keeping in mind classical topic models (i.e., LDA)
(D. M. Blei, Ng, and Jordan 2003) and Correlated Topic Modelling (CTM)
(D. M. Blei and Lafferty 2007). While LDA assumes that topics are
independent, CTM relaxes this assumption by allowing topics to be
correlated. This approach can be suitable for the cases where certain
topics have the tendency to co-occur. The particularity of STM is that
it allows users to add extra information about each document to be
analyzed with the topic modeling framework. This is known as metadata
addition. This procedure helps the marketer discover topics and link
them to covariates represented by the related document metadata because
topical prevalence and topical content can be a function of them
(Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019). Technically, metadata that
influences the popularity of topics is referred to as topical prevalence
covariates, and variables (words) that define the theme of topics are
called topical content covariates.

In this section we will use the R package \texttt{stm} (Roberts,
Stewart, and Tingley 2019). The idea is to use the functions in the
package, on the textual data and the associated metadata. In our case,
it can be the review as the text, the associated brand, the review's
date, and it's rating, as metadata. We can use the \texttt{quanteda}
package to import text and metadata, pre-process and transform the
document into a document-term matrix. From the \texttt{prodreviews}
variable that contains the dataset, we select the Content, Brand,
Review\_date, and Rating columns. The function \texttt{prepDocuments()}
uses the package \texttt{tm}to pre-process data.

methods, \textbf{Highest Prob} lists the most frequent words within each
topic and also some common words, reducing specificity.
\textbf{FREX}(\textbf{FR}equency and \textbf{EX}clusivity) identifies

distinctive. The words that rank high in FREX are frequent in the topic
and unique to it, similar to the TF-IDF approach. \textbf{Lift}
identifies words that are specifically common to one topic compared to
the entire corpus, it can highlight unique terms for smaller topics.
\textbf{Score} uses a combination of log frequency inside one topic and
penalizes the word's log frequency across all topics; the goal is to
find words that are common in a specific topic but uncommon elsewhere.

\subsection{Estimating metadata-topic
relationships}\label{estimating-metadata-topic-relationships}

The main function in the \texttt{stm} package for evaluating the link
between the derived topics and metadata is \texttt{estimateEffect()}.
For the \(K\) topics, it identifies the relationships and the associated
uncertainty by employing, generally, the topical prevalence model used
in estimating STM. The topical prevalence model is simlar to a
regression model, topic proportions represent the dependent variable,
while metadata (e.g., \texttt{Brand}, \texttt{Review\_date}, and
\texttt{Rating}) are the independent variables that explain how the
prevalene of topics varies across documents. The function can take as
input some selected topics and a link formula for the metadata. There
are several options for estimating this link function. To speed up
processing time, one can choose \texttt{uncertainty\ =\ "None"}, but it
this will not yield the estimation uncertainty or, \texttt{"Global"}
which considers the estimation uncertainty of the topic proportions
(Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019). The estimated relationships can be
useful in interpreting and validating the derived topics Grimmer and
Stewart (2013). First, we need to transform the \texttt{Brand} column
into a categorical variable, in R called factor. Then we need to
estimate the effect of the covariates, in this specific case we have the
\texttt{Brand}, the \texttt{Review\_date}, and the product's
\texttt{Rating} provided in a document ranging from 1 to 5 in the
\texttt{productsFit} topic model. The function \texttt{s()} applies a
spline to the \texttt{Review\_date} variable. This allows us to capture
non-linear trends over time. The role of a spline is to create a smooth
curve that models variations in topic prevalence as a continuous,
non-linear function applied to a variable, in our case
\texttt{Review\_date}. The \texttt{summary()} function gives a summary
of the estimated effect for a topic of choice.

\subsection{Determining the number of topics with
STM.}\label{determining-the-number-of-topics-with-stm.}

As it is specified also in the \texttt{stm} package manual (Roberts,
Stewart, and Tingley 2019), there is no clear answer on how to decide on
the best number of topics for a given corpus (Grimmer and Stewart 2013).
While the \texttt{stm()} function accepts a fixed number for modeling
the topics, function \texttt{searchK()} uses a data-driven approach to
selecting the number of topics. The function will perform several
automated tests to help the analyst choosing the number of topics. For
example, one could estimate a STM model from 3 to 7 topics and compare
the results along each of the criteria. The default initialization is
the spectral initialization due to its stability. This function will
also calculate a range of quantities of interest, such as average
exclusivity, semantic coherence, held-out likelihood, residuals, bound,
lower bound, and EM iterations. Exclusivity generally increases with the
number of topics (K), indicating more distinct topics. Semantic
coherence decreases with (K), with higher coherence suggesting more
meaningful topics and some more difficulty to interpret topics. The
held-out likelihood becomes less negative as (K) increases, with higher
values being better. Residuals decrease with increasing (K), with lower
residuals being preferable. The bound (or lower bound) increases with
(K), where higher values are advantageous, and leading to a better model
fitting the data. EM iterations vary, and typically, fewer iterations
indicate better convergence, but this depends on other factors too.

topics at \(K=7\) are more distinct than at \(K=6\). Despite having the
lowest semantic coherence (the topics may be less interpretable), it
performs well on the held-out likelihood, showing the least-negative
value (meaning that it generalizes unseen data better), residuals (best
fit to the data), and bound (best in both high and low). The number of a
lower EM iterations indicates that the model is stable and not
overfitted. So \(K=7\) appears to offer a good balance among the
metrics, if data fit and generalization are important for predictive
modeling or explanatory purposes, otherwise, one can choose \(K=5\) for
more meaningful and easily understood topics if interpretability and
semantic coherence are important.

There ore other ways to select the number of topics, for example using
LDA tuning. This might be an useful tool for optimizing topic modeling
performance by selecting an optimal number of topics. By tuning LDA
parameters, we can obtain models that represent better the topics in the
corpus, allowing the analyst to interpret the results and draw
actionable insights. The \texttt{ldatuning} package (Nikita and Nikita
2016) allows for analyzing some metrics that either reflect minimization
Cao et al. (2009), or maximization (Griffiths and Steyvers 2004). We
load the dataset inside R, we preprocess it, then we apply several
metrics, such as ``Griffiths2004'', ``CaoJuan2009'', ``Arun2010'', and a
method, such as ``Gibbs''.

The obtain topics of high quality we need to consider several factors.
One of them is the quality and quantity of the corpus and its
pre-processing steps. Another one is the real variety of topics in the
corpus. The number of grams (n-grams) considered, may influence the
context and coherence of topics, here we have worked with 1-grams but 2
or more grams add value to the final results. Last but not least, the
fine-tuning of parameters, optimizing model performance for better
accuracy and insights.

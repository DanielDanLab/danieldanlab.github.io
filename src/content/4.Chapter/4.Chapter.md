Computers work with numbers. They are incredibly efficient at this task,
which is what they were initially built for: mathematical computations.
Gradually, some of the real-world mechanics have been adapted and
shifted to digital use. When we think about typing text, although there
were once plenty of typewriters, we have now switched to computers which
allow us to lay text on a white canvas of pixels that mimics a sheet of
paper. While the vast majority of people write text, just a tiny
fraction analyze it, and the latter is our job here. But in text
analytics, text must be made ready for interpretation by computer, and
we have the duty to ensure this process is done properly. We act at a
higher level and do not see the transformation take place, but we must
follow specific strategies and procedures to convert text documents into
vectors of numbers. This mapping between a sequence of symbols and
digits is called feature representation, feature extraction, or feature
encoding (Goldberg 2017).

Before we look at deeper meanings or more elaborate forms of analysis,
there is much basic information that can be extracted from text, such as
the number of words, the number of characters, the number of special
characters, the number of numbers, the average word length, and so on.
While all these features can be extracted, a widely used conceptual
model of text known as bag-of-words (BoW) focuses on the frequency of
words in a text. For applications of this concept to marketing, see, for
example, Netzer, Lemaire, and Herzenstein (2019) and Toubia et al.
(2019). The name bag implies that the words are simply thrown into a
container with no specific order, and this is a fitting description.
Whenever we apply this simple model, our attention is directed
exclusively to the occurrence of the words and their frequency, but not
to the structure of the text. More details on this model will be
discussed below. Curiously enough, by dismantling the text's
structure---removing and modifying words or parts of them and
eliminating punctuation marks---we can gain insights without having to
read hundreds of pages simply by analyzing a selection of its
(unordered) words. Using the techniques presented in the previous
chapter, we will start by collecting a simple text from the internet and
transferring it to R, and then conducting some very basic analysis on
it. After that we will use the dataset mentioned in
Chapter [\[introduction-1\]](#introduction-1){reference-type="ref"
reference="introduction-1"}, apply initial reasoned text-processing
steps, and transform it into a matrix, enabling our computer to handle
it efficiently. Finally, we will analyze the outcome. What we are trying
to achieve is size reduction. We are aiming to filter information and
extract knowledge from huge numbers of documents. To accomplish this, we
have to cut and remove parts of the text, leaving only the essential
(and meaningful) elements.

## Data preparation and normalization (text pre-processing) {#sec-data-preparation-and-normalization-text-preprocessing}

To summarize, collecting text is the starting point of the whole
process. Given that text must be structured and made ready for computer
analysis, it is essential to normalize, or pre-process, it (Meyer,
Hornik, and Feinerer 2008; Miner et al. 2012). Following text correction
using regular expressions (see
Chapter [\[textual-data\]](#textual-data){reference-type="ref"
reference="textual-data"}), the main preliminary operations are: (i)
corpus preparation, (ii) lowercase transformation, (iii) stop-word
removal, (iv) implicit-word removal, (v) punctuation removal, (vi)
number removal or number-to-text translation, and vice versa, (vii)
inflectional-form reduction, and, (viii) white-space contraction. We
demonstrate each of them below in the sequence in which they will be
applied.

### Corpus preparation

For out text mining procedures we will use the `tm` package: This is an
abbreviation of **t**ext **m**ining. The functions in this section refer
to this, unless specified otherwise. The `tm` package works with a
structure called `Corpus` (note that the plural of corpus is corpora).
This is an abstract concept consisting of a collection of texts that are
to be analyzed. By default `tm` uses a volatile corpus, or `VCorpus`,
which is a structure held in memory in an R object and which vanishes
when destroyed.

In R, the function `options()` allows you to modify a number of default
values, one of them is `stringsAsFactors` in which the default is set to
`TRUE` and turns strings that appear more than once into factors.[^1]

In this example, we are working with the dataset detailed in the
introductory chapter and available for download from the book's website.
To import this dataset, which is in CSV format, we use the `read.csv()`
function. If you have not followed the setup instructions in the
preparatory chapter, you might have a different path for where you store
your dataset, so please check that everything is in place to prevent
errors. The dataset counts several columns, but we will focus mainly on
the one containing the reviews and store it in a variable called
`prodreviews`. The next step is to convert the 'Content' column to

When you have a vector where each element is a different product review,

### Lowercase transformation

For humans, the distinction between the words *hotel* and *Hotel* is
minimal and anyway, they both refer to lodgings. For computers this is
different. Words have no semantic meaning to them, are just a sequence
of letters, and if two words differ only through capitalization, then
the two are distinct. Converting all words to lowercase ensures
uniformity. This enables the accurate counting of words. Converting to
uppercase is also an option but the text becomes less readable and, in
online communication, is interpreted as shouting. Remember, collecting
accurate word-based statistics is essential in the BoW model. The
function that can accomplish this is `tolower()` and is part of the R
base program. There is an important technical detail to note, while
`tm_map()` is designed to work with corpus objects, using it with some
standard R functions directly can alter the structure of the content in
the corpus. To ensure that the content keeps its proper format, such as
`PlainTextDocument`, we use the `content_transformer()` wrapper
function. By wrapping `tolower()` with `content_transformer()`, we make
sure that the text is converted to lowercase while maintaining the
structure of the corpus.

### Stop-word removal {#stop-words-removal}

You should already be aware that the most frequent word in English
texts, is the article *the*. Comparing the word frequencies of two
different texts with different topics, we would notice that the first 50
words would be likely to have similar frequencies. Such words that
appear very frequently but are also uninformative, we repeat this again,
are called stop words (Fox 1989). Once the `tm` library is loaded, a
complete list of the stop words can be found by typing `stopwords()` in
R's console. As you can notice, the list includes contracted form such
as *isn't*. This tells us that we need to remove the stop words
**before** eliminating the punctuation. To remove the stop words (and
others if needed) from our text, we use the `removeWords()` function.

example, `3` into `three`) or to remove them completely. A function can
be easily created to map between numbers and text, if such a
transformation is necessary. The function `removeNumbers()` will simply
remove all numerical characters from the text.

### Reduction of inflectional forms {#sec-reduction-of-inflectional-forms}

To reduce inflectional forms in a text, processes like stemming (Lovins
1968) and lemmatization (Plisson et al. 2004; Toman, Tesar, and Jezek
2006; Chai 2023) are commonly used. A stemming algorithm refers to a
rule-based heuristic computational process that aims to make words
uniform by reducing them to a common root (or a common stem), depending
on whether we remove the prefix or the suffix. For example, a stemmer
would reduce words such as *marketing*, *marketed*, or *marketization*
to the word *market*. The goal is to achieve this correctly, hopefully
most of the time. The `tm` package uses the `SnowballC` (Bouchet-Valat
and Bouchet-Valat 2020) package for stemming which implements Martin
Porter's version of the stemming algorithm (1980) which focuses on
suffix removal rather than prefix removal. This version has become
standard for stemming in English. Be aware that the algorithm does not
reduce words to a known stem word but to a neologism, for example, the
words *replacement*, *replace*, or *replacing* might be reduced to
*replac*. The function is `stemDocument()`. Stemming is suitable for
applications such as simple search engines, or, as in our case,
preliminary text analysis.

After all these steps, we have reached a point where we need to
introduce text representation, a transformation that converts words and
phrases into numbers or vectors, enabling computers to process textual
data. This numerical representation is essential for various methods of
text analysis, such as topic modeling, sentiment analysis, and machine
learning algorithms applied to text data. We will analyze all of these
in the following chapters.

## Text representation

Text representation is linked to a language model. But what exactly is a
model? It is a simplified and interpretative representation of reality
which selectively emphasizes certain features, but is not reality. For
example, in geography a model could be a map, for transportation a train
schedule, in music the notation, in finance or marketing graphs and/or
diagrams showing hypothetical relationships between variables, such as
ad spend vs. sales. Models can do more than just describe reality, they
can also predict or forecast phenomena (e.g., weather forecasts), or
reveal patterns (e.g., seasonality in sales data). Mathematical models
might show predictions of weather patterns or the understanding of
latent information such as the seasonality of sales through a
time-series graph. In the context of climate change, models can provide
projections based on current data trends, such as $\text{CO}_2$
emissions. Statistical models are used to summarize data and reveal
patterns. These models use observations to extract and infer
information, helping with tasks such as classifying or predicting. In a
statistical language model (or simply a language model), when you have a
set of documents (a corpus), the model estimates the probability that
the text in the documents was generated by that model (Aggarwal 2018).
It is important to note that models cannot perfectly mirror reality.
They are abstractions which come laden with limitations and assumptions.
For example, a map sacrifices details of terrain for simplicity, and a
language model may miss nuanced contextual meanings.

A statistical language model also implies that there are mathematical
operations, so the text needs to be transformed into mathematical
values, shifting from unstructured to structured tabular formats. Like
all statistical models, a language model (LM), with a specified language
such as English, is designed to assign probabilities to phrases. More
precisely, LMs are designed to calculate the probability or "the
likelihood of a given word (or a sequence of words) to follow a sequence
of words" Beumer (2020). A simple but frequently used model to represent
text is the vector space model (VSM). A VSM encodes words into numerical
vectors, in a similar way to how a map shows territory. However, VSMs
treat words as independent dimensions, which oversimplifies semantic
relationships (e.g., synonyms are not inherently linked with each
other). Let us see what we can work with: We have *terms*, which are
basically words after they have been processed (e.g., \"running\" into
\"run\"), *documents*, which can be phrases, paragraphs, articles,
chapters, or books, and *corpora*, which are a collection of these
documents.

### Vector space model {#sec-vsm-bow}

In a VSM, the mapping is structured and gives mathematical meaning to
the terms, documents, and the corpus. We know that the terms in a
document appear with a certain frequency, which we can call weight and
denote with a $w$. A single document can be denoted by a $d$. So, a
document $i$ in a collection of documents $D$ that contains $N$ unique
terms ($N$ is the vocabulary's dimension) can be represented
mathematically as a vector: $$d_i=(w_1, w_2, ..., w_j, ..., w_N)$$ where
$1 \leq j \leq N$ and $i \in 1...D$ (Jurafsky and Martin 2008)

For example, a simple document $d_1$ which contains the words "The more
the better" can be represented by $d_1=(1,1,2)$ corresponding to
("better", "more", "the"). This is a vectorial representation of the
text. If you put all these vectors together, one beneath the other, the
final result is a matrix. In the rows you will have the documents in the
corpus. In the columns you will have the words in the vocabulary. Some
words might appear in some documents but not in others, some might
appear in all the documents and some might appear in only one. This is
the distribution of the words over the documents. The matrix is our main
structure from which we extract features. Maybe the simplest technique
in this model is the Bag-of-Words (BoW), where the representation of the
words is 1-gram, meaning that we consider one word at a time. There are
also other representations based on n-grams, where $n$ can take any
number $\geq 2$. In the BoW approach, it is assumed that the important
unit is the word, so structure and order can be discarded. As such, the
semantics of a document are given by the frequency of the words used,
and the similitude with other documents is based on the similitude of
word frequencies (Services 2015). BoW performs well at representing and
comparing the contents of documents.

To all intents and purposes, after the normalization process, we have
developed a BoW model. Despite its name, hinting that it is baggy, we
still need a structure for it to do computational tasks. This structure
has a grid form and is called the document-term matrix (`dtm`) (Hofmann
1999). The `dtm` is a matrix with two dimensions, (*r,c*) whose rows,
*r*, represent the documents (in our case each parcel of review content)
and the columns, *c*, the terms in the vocabulary. Note that the
vocabulary contains the total number of words that appear in the whole
corpus arranged alphabetically. In the `dtm`, the frequency of a term's
occurrence in a document is represented by `n` in the respective row and
column. A value of 0 indicates the absence of a term. The value *n* is
given by the different representations of the text and there are three
ways to show it: one-hot encoding, frequency vectors (or count vectors),
and TF-IDF vectors.

#### TF-IDF

A model that which acquired a good reputation is the TF-IDF measure.
This is used in text analysis for document comparison or for text
classification, but its origins were rooted in text information
retrieval long before computers were even popular (Jones 1972, 1973;
Aizawa 2003). Most importantly, it compares documents and shows which is
more important based on specific words, in a very similar way to how a
search engine prioritizes information retrieval based on search terms.
The reason why TF-IDF works well with a BoW approach,

The basic use of TF-IDF is to assess the importance of a term in a
document. This is based on statistical principles. Remember, statistics
is the branch of mathematics that, among many other operations, also
analyzes the frequency of a (past) event. So the more important a term
is, the 'heavier', or more frequent, it is expected to be.

To get an intuitive understanding, of how the algorithm works, note that
it is composed of two parts, the TF (**T**erm **f**requency) and the IDF
(**i**nverse **d**ocument **f**requency). TF quantifies how frequently a
term appears in a specific document relative to all other words in that
document: the more frequent a word is, the higher the TF. Conversely,
IDF instead the rarity of a word in **a corpus of documents**. A word
that appears in fewer documents is more significant because it is rare,
and thus has a higher IDF value. The product of TF and IDF gives us a
score (or a weight) for each word, ranking its importance with respect
to the other words. Let us look at an example and examine the procedure
in detail. Consider two sentences:

-   Great tablet for the price.

-   The best tablet.

The sentences contain 5 and 3 terms respectively, excluding punctuation.
We organize them alphabetically because it facilitates tracking and
searching for specific terms. The collection of all the terms in these
sentences forms the vocabulary.

In Table [1.1](#tbl-tfidf){reference-type="ref" reference="tbl-tfidf"}
we see the vector representation of the two sentences. The header row
contains the terms. The first row shows the number of documents in which
each term appears. The second and the third rows contain the relative
**T**erm **f**requency for the first and the second documents
respectively. The word term, $t$, is the same as the word, $w$. The
formula to calculate **TF** is
$TF_{(t,d)}=\frac{f_{t,d}}{\sum_{t^{'}\in d}f_{t^{'},d}}$. This means
the term frequency of *t* in document *d* is the ratio of the term's
frequency *f* in that document to the total number of terms in the
document. That total is the sum of all the terms
$\sum_{t^{'}\in d}f_{t^{'},d}$ present in a document *d*. In our case,
for the first sentence (which we consider a document) there are a total
of 7 words. The word "the" appears twice, so its (relative) frequency,
**TF**, is $\frac{2}{7}$. The fourth row represents the **i**nverse
**d**ocument **f**requency for each word in the vocabulary. Its
simplified formula (simplified because sometimes after the $log()$ a 1
is added to avoid edge cases in text processing) is
$IDF_{(t)}=log(\frac{N}{n_{t}})$, where $N$ represents the total number
of the documents, in our case 2, and $n_{t}$ is the number of documents
in which the term appears (see row one). For example, the word "the"
appears in both documents, so $n_{t}=2$. From algebra we know how to
compute $log(\frac{2}{2})=log(1)=0$. The fifth and the sixth rows
represent the multiplication of each document's **TF** with the
corresponding **IDF**.

There are several ways you can calculate **TF-IDF** but the principle
remains the same: for the TF, the more frequent a term, the more
important it is, see Beel, Langer, and Gipp (2017). In IDF, the rarer a
term is in the corpus (i.e., among all the documents), the more
important it is. The word "the" will appear many times so it will have a
high TF because it is the most-used term in the English language. But
this word will be penalized in the IDF because it appears in all the
documents: the more it appears the lower the score, so it has an inverse
(document) frequency.

What TF-IDF accomplishes is to find the words that are important to a
document in a corpus. While TF relates to the first part of a BoW
approach, the TF-IDF metric will compute which words characterize the
individual documents. To sum up, a word that appears frequently in one
or just a few documents, will have a high **TF$\times$IDF** score.

While TF-IDF can exist as a standalone algorithm, it can also be applied
to a BoW by replacing the word counts with their TF-IDF values. The
resulting matrix becomes a weighted version of BoW, where each value
indicates the importance of a term in a document relative to the whole
corpus. In simple terms, while BoW focuses solely on frequency, TF-IDF
balances frequency with importance. This is why the initial stage in the
BoW model is essential, because it allows the TF-IDF matrix to emphasize
words that are both informative and contextually relevant.

### Size reduction

Now that we explored the purpose of preparing the text and how the VSM
quantifies words, an important further step before we move on to more
advanced techniques to analyze text is size reduction. By now it is
evident that the matrices that are created through a VSM can be huge,
depending on the number of documents and the breadth of vocabulary.
These matrices are often sparse. meaning that they contain a lot of zero
values due to the absence of most words in most documents.

In specific cases, infrequent words do not bring much insight, and when
this happens they can be removed. However, in other cases the words have
to remain because, as you could see in the TF-IDF model, they might
characterize a document or enrich a text. This might give us some
insights and we suggest not to dismiss the possibility of unearthing
knowledge from them. The words that appear only once in a corpus are
technically called *hapax legomena*, or *hapax* for short. The longer
the text, the higher the probability you will see an increase in *hapax*
(Jockers and Thalken 2020a).

#### Sparse-terms removal

Following data preparation and normalization (see
Section [1.2](#sec-data-preparation-and-normalization-text-preprocessing){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-data-preparation-and-normalization-text-preprocessing"}),
another procedure involves removing the sparse terms from the `dtm`.
These are terms that appear very infrequently in a document and are
recognized in a `dtm` by the fact that it contains many entries set to
zero in the $j^{th}$ column and just a few entries greater than zero. To
remove them we can use the sparse option from the `removeSparseTerms()`
function. The 'sparsity' parameter here refers to a frequency threshold
beyond which terms are discarded. Sparsity decreases as it approaches
1.0 and can hold values in the open interval (0, 1.0).

Let us take an example. If we look at the sample, notice the abundance
of zeros in the matrix. If the dataset was larger, words like "ability"
or "access" would appear more frequently, but there would still be a lot
of zeros in the columns. If a term does not have a lot of values (again,
in the columns), then the sparsity is increased, bringing the index
close to 1, meaning that there are a lot of zeros. If the word is
present in all the documents, for example the word "tablet", then it
will fill out its column with values different from zero, making the
sparsity index close to 0. We can conclude by saying that the sparsity
index essentially acts as a threshold, eliminating terms whose
proportion of zeros exceeds a specified level. For large corpora, a
threshold of between $0.95$ and a value just below $1$ is commonly used.

#### Word correlation

Words in a text are related. They are linked to other words to create
meaning. To get deeper insights into our data, we can check for
correlations between some of the most frequent terms and the other words
in the document set (Kwartler 2017). By correlation, in this context, we
mean a measure of the words that co-occur in a document set. This can
indicate a relationship or association between the terms. To analyze
correlation you can use the **findAssocs()** function from the **tm**
package. The function requires you to specify the document-term matrix
and the correlation threshold which determines the minimum strength of
association to be considered significant, and is set to a number between
0 and 1. If we set a correlation limit of $0.10$, the function will
return words that co-occur with the target word with a correlation score
of **at least** $0.10$. This number represents the likelihood of the
words appearing together relative to their occurring independently. From
an analytical point of view, using this we can unveil potential thematic
or contextual connections within the documents. The correlation score
computed by `findAssocs()` is based on Pearson's correlation
coefficient. This evaluates the linear relationship between the
frequency vector of the target term and the frequency vectors of other
terms in the document-term matrix. This method highlights terms that
exhibit a significant linear relationship with the target word.

#### Bi-gram word clouds {#bigram-wordclouds}

In a VSM, text can be easily tokenized as single words, having a 1-gram
representation. We can also use 2-grams, commonly known as bi-grams. You
can have even longer combinations, called n-grams. A bi-gram word cloud
uses pairs of words as tokens, providing more insights into which terms
are used together in reviews. A library that helps tokenization is
**RWeka** (Hornik, Buchta, and Zeileis 2009), and allows for the
creation of n-gram models.

#### Community Detection

You can also attempt to perform community detection on the previous
graph. This is an important tool for uncovering patterns, clusters, or
hidden structures represented by words that have a higher probability of
appearing together in some groups than other groups. To understand more
about this type of analysis, see Fortunato and Hric (2016). These
communities often represent thematic or topical groupings in texts,
providing insights into the underlying structure and content of the
corpus. There are several community-detection methods, such as
Girvan-Newman, infomap, or label propagation (Lancichinetti and
Fortunato 2009) but a well-known one due to its fast parsing of large
datasets is the so-called Louvain method (Blondel et al. 2008). In word
networks, this is a popular community-detection tool used to identify
clusters of words that frequently co-occur in a corpus. It iteratively
groups nodes (words) into communities, so that the modularity is
maximized, revealing significant word clusters that might represent
specific topics or themes.

An interesting application of it for marketing is in market basket
analysis (Faridizadeh, Abdolvand, and Rajaee Harandi 2018). This allows
businesses to promote their products more successfully and it is
possible to discover communities with special properties, gleaning
knowledge from shopping behavior. In the case of textual data, we obtain
extra knowledge from how customers put words together in their feedback
and on which aspects they frequently focus.

### Marketing applications

How does the frequency of the words give insights into a set of
documents and how can this be used for marketing purposes? The frequency
of the words indicates that there is a tendency to describe a
phenomenon, following the argument that the more you talk about
something, the more important it is. But those words are perhaps the
ones that are also used in a search engine, so employing them, or at
least considering them as SEO keywords, is as important as checking
trends with tools such as Google Trends. You can be found on the
internet if people look for you, so these words can work as hooks. But
how many words you should choose for SEO? Knowing that the competition
for one or two words is tight (see
Figure [1.6](#fig-longtail){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-longtail"}), the long tail (Anderson 2013) is a very
helpful place to examine. Check the search-engine results page and you
will see that Google's recent advances in NLP allow you to use a more
generous number of words.

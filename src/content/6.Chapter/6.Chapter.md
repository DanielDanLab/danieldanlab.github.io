# Classification

The term 'class' originates from the Latin 'classis,' which was used in
ancient Rome to categorize citizens within the centuriate assembly based
on wealth and property. This system, attributed to the reforms of
Servius Tullius, the sixth king of Rome, divided society into several
classes. The wealthiest in the upper classes were equipped for heavy
infantry and granted significant political power. In contrast, the lower
classes consisted of those with less wealth, serving as light infantry
or not armed, with limited political influence. This classification
primarily determined military duties and voting rights, reflecting an
organization of society by economic status. Later, this classification
of individual citizens was used for taxation purposes. The assignment of
a citizen to one of the five existing classes involves several features,
such as the armor type possessed, wealth, age, and a few others.
Classification for taxation and not for military reasons was important
because paying tributes was a constant need, while wars did not occur
regularly (Cornell 2012).

Some two thousand five hundred years fast forward, we have developed
several ways to accomplish the task of classification, one of them is
manual classification. Due to the incredible amount of data, it is very
impractical for timely processing. In Chapter 5 we explored a method
known as clustering. Both classification and clustering aim to achieve
the same goal, put data into categories through algorithms. Clustering
uses Unsupervised Learning, where the algorithm, with the help of
mathematical formulas, attempts to group elements with common
characteristics without predefined categories. This means we interpret
the categories after the algorithm has done its work.

On the other hand, classification involves Supervised Learning. Here, we
start with a training set, a collection of documents already categorized
into classes or categories. The algorithm learns from this existing
categorization to create a model, or a 'modus operandi,' to categorize
new data. This process is essential when data needs to be sorted
automatically to save time and achieve accurate results.

For instance, a Customer Relationship Manager dealing with hundreds of
incoming emails daily could use an automated system to sort these emails
into the correct departments, such as : Refunds, Logistics, Complaints,
and many more. Another classical application in the direct marketing
context is RFM classification, which entails segmenting customers based
on the Recency, the Frequency, and the Monetary value of their previous
interactions with a focal firm (see, e.g., Bult and Wansbeek (1995),
Gönül, Kim, and Shi (2000), Berry and Linoff (2009)). The applications
of automatic classification are vast.

This chapter will focus on classification using the same tablet dataset
we used in the previous chapters. Note that there is a 'Brand' column at
the end of the dataset, which we will use for classification. Each
review in the dataset is associated with a brand. The goal is for every
new review, which lacks a brand classification, to be processed by the
model and assigned to the correct brand category.

Regarding the types of classification algorithms, this chapter will
cover Linear Discriminant Analysis, K-Nearest Neighbors, Support Vector
Machines, and Neural Networks. To complete the chapter, we will use a
library, FastText, used for efficient learning of word representations
and sentence classification. It is optimal for its speed of execution
and accuracy, especially with large datasets.

## Supervised Machine Learning Algorithm Models

In this section, we will focus on employing Supervised Machine Learning
algorithms. Our primary elements are the text content of the review and
its association with a specific brand. Utilizing these well-understood
elements, the algorithm will generate a model. This model enables the
classification of new, unlabeled content, ensuring that the correct
brand label is accurately assigned.

The entire process is depicted in
Figure [1.1](#fig-ml){reference-type="ref" reference="fig-ml"},
illustrating a typical Machine Learning workflow applied to a corpus of
documents. This procedure remains consistent across different types of
datasets. Initially (step I in
Figure [1.1](#fig-ml){reference-type="ref" reference="fig-ml"}), we
provide the algorithm with a set of documents that have already been
classified, each review tagged with a brand label. This dataset is known
as the training dataset, offering the algorithm a comprehensive
framework on how reviews correlate with brands. Through the analysis of
this association and specific classification rules, the machine
constructs a model based on the input data, particularly the words
within each review.

In the subsequent step II, we introduce a collection of unlabeled
documents to the previously established model. The expectation is for
these documents to be accurately classified according to the model's
guidelines. Computers excel when identifying regularities within data,
leveraging these patterns for efficient and precise predictions.
Essentially, this is a prediction task: given a set of reviews, the
machine will, with a certain degree of probability, predict the
appropriate brand assignment.

The algorithm's accuracy is evaluated using a testing set, typically
comprising 20
Figure [1.1](#fig-ml){reference-type="ref" reference="fig-ml"}, this
evaluation is an integral part of the workflow. After the training phase
(step I), the classification model is applied to the testing set (step
II), which is processed by the model as if it were unlabeled. The
outcomes of this classification are then compared against the actual
labels (ground-truth values). While the train-test split is commonly
80:20, other configurations are also possible, such as a training,
validation, and test split (e.g., 80:10:10). In these setups, the
validation set is used to fine-tune the model, while the test set is
reserved exclusively for evaluating its performance. The higher the
congruence between the algorithm's assignments and the original labels,
the greater the algorithm's accuracy.

Can algorithms guarantee flawless performance? Not with the current
state of technology. However, they are efficient enough to significantly
reduce manual effort. When data are misclassified, human intervention
becomes necessary for manual correction. A robust approach includes
regularly updating the training dataset after correcting any errors.
This process not only introduces new vocabulary and scenarios to the
model but also enhances the accuracy of its predictions.

Classification algorithms vary in effectiveness, with some outperforming
others based on the specific requirements of the data and the context of
its application. Broadly, we can categorize these algorithms into two
types: linear and nonlinear. Linear classification uses a straight line
(or hyperplane in higher dimensions) to divide data into distinct
classes, which works well for linearly separable data. On the other
hand, nonlinear classification employs more complex boundaries to
separate classes, enabling it to handle data with intricate patterns or
overlaps.

### Linear Discriminant Analysis

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) is a robust algorithm for
classification tasks, especially when distinguishing between more than
two classes. While logistic regression, which is another algorithm, is
widely used for binary classification (Komarek 2004), it faces
challenges with multiclass scenarios, where Multinomial Logistic
Regression or LDA become more suitable (Torkkola 2001). In marketing,
both techniques are standard when the task is to (post-hoc) describe
previously derived market segments (which are built by using some type
of clustering method); for an overview we refer to Wedel and Kamakura
(2000) or Reutterer and Dan (2021). The word "discriminate" means to
separate and LDA utilizes a linear discriminant function to separate
classes, addressing some of the limitations of logistic regression, such
as instability in cases of well-separated classes or small datasets. It
is important to distinguish LDA from another, non-related
soft-clustering algorithm called Latent Dirichlet Allocation despite
sharing the same abbreviation. We will discuss this one in the next
chapter. Classification LDA assumes that the variables follow a Normal
distribution and that variances are equal across groups
(homoscedasticity), making outliers a potential issue, as with many
models (Balakrishnama and Ganapathiraju 1998).

Several algorithms extend LDA's principles, including Quadratic
Discriminant Analysis (Tharwat 2016), Flexible Discriminant Analysis
(Hastie, Tibshirani, and Buja 1994), and Regularized Discriminant
Analysis (Friedman 1989). In machine learning, model parameters are
estimated from training data to make predictions. The caret package in
R, standing for **C**lassification **A**nd **RE**gression **T**raining,
facilitates this process with functions for data splitting,
preprocessing, feature selection, model tuning through cross-validation,
resampling, and estimating variable importance (Kuhn, Johnson, et al.
2013). For model tuning, caret's `trainControl()` function is versatile,
allowing for various validation techniques, including K-fold
cross-validation, a method we explore in the context of LDA.
Cross-validation divides the training set into K segments, or folds,
typically between 5 and 10, to iteratively train and validate the model,
enhancing its reliability and performance (Forman and Scholz 2010).

of folds. In every iteration, a model is trained on the training set and
then applied to the validation set for prediction. The discrepancy
between the model's prediction and the ground-truth labels of the
validation fold is measured as misclassification. The overall error rate
**E** is calculated as the average of the errors from each iteration
*i*. A common misunderstanding is the belief that the best-performing
model from these iterations is selected for future classification tasks.
However, this approach does not fully align with best practices.
Cross-validation serves as a method to estimate how well the algorithm
is likely to perform on unseen data. It provides a measure of the
model's generalizability rather than selecting a specific model for use.
It is also important to note that the test set remains untouched during
cross-validation and is reserved exclusively for final evaluation.

The final model, intended for operational use, is trained on the entire
training dataset without folding using the hyper-parameters chosen (or
validated) during cross-validation. Once this model is developed, it can
then be evaluated on the test set, with performance metrics providing
insight into its effectiveness with the help of a confusion matrix. A
confusion matrix is represented in tabular form and used to evaluate the
performance of classification models. It categorizes predictions into
four categories: true positives (correct positive predictions), false
positives (incorrectly predicted as positive), false negatives
(incorrectly predicted as negative), and true negatives (correct
negative predictions). When class distribution is imbalanced, it is
common to complement overall accuracy with metrics such as precision,
recall, and F1-score.

#### A word about Accuracy

Assessing what constitutes "good" accuracy in machine learning is
nuanced, influenced by factors such as the application's specifics,
problem complexity, and class distribution within the dataset. A
fundamental measure, baseline accuracy, represents the success rate of
the most naive model that always predicts the most frequent class. For a
model to be considered effective, its accuracy must substantially exceed
this baseline, indicating genuine learning rather than mere replication
of existing class distributions. Simple, well-defined problems might
warrant accuracies approaching perfection, whereas the messiness of
real-world data, characterized by noise and class overlap, stops these
expectations for more complex issues. The presence of imbalanced classes
further complicates matters, rendering high overall accuracy potentially
deceptive. Here, metrics such as precision, recall, and the F1 score,
particularly in relation to minority class performance, offer a better
assessment of a model's efficacy. Moreover, the benchmark for "good"
accuracy is not universal but varies across domains. For example, the
thresholds for acceptability in spam detection versus medical
diagnostics diverge significantly due to the differing consequences of
errors.

### K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classification

The K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm is simple and effective in both
classification and regression tasks. Classification with KNN aims to
assign entities to predefined categories, whereas regression predicts a
numerical value. Our focus here will be on its application in
classification.

A unique classification of KNN is its operational mechanism, nothing is
pretrained before the model is used. This means that, unlike algorithms
that learn parameters to make predictions for classification, KNN relies
on the dataset itself, identifying the $K$ most similar instances (or
"neighbors") to a given query point to make a prediction. New data are
evaluated during the runtime against the pre-existing data. The concept
of "similarity" is quantified through distance metrics, making KNN a
distance-based algorithm, and the Euclidean distance is an excellent
candidate.

When the algorithm needs to make a prediction for a new instance, KNN
searches through the training dataset (i.e., the pre-existing data) to
find the $K$ nearest instances (neighbors) to the new data instance. The
prediction is then made by assigning the new instance to one of the
predefined categories, based on the majority class among its nearest
neighbors.

Determining the optimal number of neighbors, $K$, is essential for the
algorithm's performance, with the best value typically identified
through experimentation and validation techniques. KNN's principle is
straightforward: classify an entity based on the majority vote of the
classes or average outcome of the target values of its $K$ nearest
neighbors (Brownlee 2016). This vote or outcome is based on the
similarity calculations between the new instance and its neighbors.
Notice that in order to derive an unanimous assignment decision, the
value for $K$ must always be odd. In case of even values, the algorithm
would not know to which class the new element should be assigned. While
KNN is known for its ease of understanding and implementation,
especially in scenarios with a small number of predictors, its
efficiency diminishes as the dimensionality of the dataset increases, a
phenomenon known as the "curse of dimensionality." Further reading is
recommended for a more detailed exploration of KNN and its applications
(e.g., Tan 2006).

### Support Vector Machines classification

Support Vector Machines (SVM) is a well-known and used classification
procedure that performs well for binary classification and in
multi-class classification scenarios (D. Cui and Curry 2005). It is
known to need little tuning in creating a model. We need to introduce
the concept of Maximal-Margin Classifier here to explain the internals
of SVM. Usually we have $n$ input variables, such as the words in a
vocabulary. Classification means separation and is done through a
hyperplane. For example, in a 3-dimensional space, a hyperplane will
have 2 dimensions while in a a 2-dimensional space the hyperplane has
1-dimension which is basically a line. For simplicity let us take a two
dimensional space. In Figure [1.3](#fig-svm){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-svm"}, we can notice the optimal solution SVM finds for
the position of the separation line for distinguishing two classes out
of an infinity of possible solutions the line can pass through.

<figure id="fig-svm">

<figcaption><span id="fig-svm" label="fig-svm"></span>Support Vector
Machines Classes Separation, Authors’ own figure</figcaption>
</figure>

The prediction model classifies new elements based on their position
relative to the decision boundary: elements on one side of the line
(where the line model equation yields a value greater than zero) are
assigned to one class, while those on the other side (with values less
than zero) are classified into the opposite class. Classification
becomes challenging for values that lie close to the decision boundary.
At the heart of this model is the distance between the decision boundary
and the nearest points on either side, belonging to both classes. The
optimal decision boundary, known as the Maximal-Margin hyperplane, is
the one that maximizes this distance, ensuring the most precise
separation between (in our case: two) classes. The points nearest to
this hyperplane are called support vectors, as they "support" the
positioning of the hyperplane, playing an essential role in defining a
robust separator. During training, the model aims to identify this
optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin for a given dataset
(Brownlee 2016).

In scenarios where perfect separation is not feasible, the Soft Margin
Classifier offers a more flexible solution. It introduces a parameter,
denoted as C (for "cost"), which adjusts the model's tolerance for
misclassification. Tuning the C parameter allows for some points to
cross the boundary, effectively balancing the trade-off between a strict
separation and allowing for misclassification. A higher C value tends to
reduce misclassification by enforcing stricter margins.

The construction of the hyperplane in SVM is achieved through linear
algebra operations known as the dot product, also referred to as the
inner product. For instance, consider two vectors represented by \[1,
2\] and \[3, 4\]; their inner product is calculated as the sum of the
products of their corresponding elements, $1\times 3 + 2\times 4$. In
SVM, the classification prediction involves an equation that utilizes
the inner product between the input feature values $(x)$ and the support
vector values $(x_i)$,

$$f(x)=b_0 + \sum(w_i*(x,x_i))$$

while $b_0$ and $w_i$ are coefficients estimated from the model.

The prediction for a new data point involves the dot product between the
input features and the support vectors. This operation, known as the
Kernel Function, essentially measures similarity, or from another
perspective, the distance between data points.

When dealing with a linear combination of input features, we employ a
linear kernel. However, to facilitate classification in scenarios where
the data are not linearly separable, inputs can be transformed into
higher dimensions using Polynomial or Radial Kernel. This technique
allows for complex separations with curved lines. We will explore the
performance of these various kernels to determine which offers the best
separation.

#### Linear SVM.

The linear SVM kernel is the simplest among the various types of kernels
used in SVM models. It excels in scenarios with many features, making it
particularly well-suited for text classification tasks. Literature Gao
and Sun (2010) suggests that linear kernels are efficient and performant
for such high-dimensional data like texts due to their straightforward
computation. Compared to more complex kernels, linear kernels are
faster, owing to their less computationally intensive nature. The
formula for the linear kernel is:

$$K(x, x_i)=\sum(x*x_i)$$ This equation represents the dot product of
the input vector $x$ and a support vector $x_i$, effectively measuring
the distance between new data points and the support vectors. The
training process for a model using a linear kernel typically involves
cross-validation, thing that you have seen before, to ensure the model
generalizes well to unseen data.

#### SVM Radial

In the context of SVM, particularly when employing the Radial Basis
Function (RBF) kernel, two critical parameters are introduced: the cost
parameter (C) and the gamma parameter $\gamma$ (sometimes referred to as
$\sigma$). The cost parameter C addresses the trade-off between
minimizing training error and ensuring model simplicity for better
generalization, essentially controlling how much misclassification is
tolerated. On the other hand, the gamma parameter $\gamma$ influences
the decision boundary's shape; lower values of $\gamma$ may result in
more linear decision boundaries, while higher values allow for more
complex, curved boundaries that can closely fit the training data. The
influence of $\gamma$ is captured in the RBF kernel formula::

$$K(x, x_i)=e^{-\gamma*\sum((x-x_i)^2)}$$ This formula illustrates how
the similarity between any two data points $x$ and $x_i$ is calculated,
with $\gamma$ modulating the impact of the distance between these points
on their calculated similarity. The values for $\gamma$ typically range
from $0<\gamma<1$ allowing for a wide spectrum of decision boundary
complexities. The radial kernel, with its ability to create complex
polygons of separation, proves to be a powerful method, especially for
datasets that are not linearly separable, enabling the model to capture
intricate patterns within the data. The default value for $\gamma$ is
$0.1$.

#### Polynomial Kernel SVM

The polynomial kernel extends the concept of the linear kernel, allowing
for more complex, non-linear decision boundaries. While it sometimes may
be less accurate or efficient compared to its linear counterpart, it
offers the flexibility to capture more complex patterns within the data.
The polynomial kernel is defined as:

$$K(x, x_i)=1+\sum(x*x_i)^d$$ Here, $d$ represents the degree of the
polynomial, which must be greater than 1 for the kernel to exhibit
non-linear properties. When $d$ = 1, the polynomial kernel simplifies to
the linear kernel. The term 'polynomial' suggests the ability to model
curved lines, enabling the separation of classes through curved
boundaries rather than straight lines. Obviously choosing different
values for $d$, can lead to more complex decision boundaries and might
better fit the training data but this will increase the risk of
overfitting. Also, a higher $d$ will result in an increased
computational complexity that affects training time and needs more
computer resources.

### Ensemble Learning

What if you could put together more than one model to obtain better
prediction accuracy and an overall higher performance? A technique that
can assist in such a situation is called Ensemble learning. As per
Oxford's dictionary definition, the word ensemble means: "a small group
of musicians, dancers or actors who perform together." We can consider
our system as an orchestra. Each machine learning model is a musician,
and each of them does its best so that all of them contribute to a
harmonious performance that outshines individual efforts.

Ensemble algorithms can be categorized into three types: Bagging,
Boosting, and Stacking. Bagging involves training multiple models on
different subsamples of the training dataset, allowing for parallel
processing and reducing variance. Boosting, on the other hand,
sequentially (like in a chain) trains models, with each one focusing on
correcting its predecessor's errors, effectively reducing bias. Unlike
the first two, Stacking combines predictions from diverse models, using
an additional model (often called a meta-model) to determine the best
way to combine these predictions, similarly to how an orchestra's
conductor ensures harmony.

Each ensemble method has its pros and cons, and the choice among them
depends on the specific challenge at hand. For further insights into
these methods, refer to (Tattar 2018). Our focus here will be on
Stacking, see an example in
Figure [1.4](#fig-ensemble){reference-type="ref"

## Neural Networks

The fascination of how our brain thinks and what can be done in computer
terms in order to mimic and outperform our reasoning found a lot of
support in the academic media. The fascination with understanding how
our brain processes information and the quest to replicate and surpass
this capability through technology has gained significant attention. The
evolution from the biological neural cells of the Natural Neural Network
to the algorithmic constructs of an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is
remarkable. ANNs, a specialized subset of machine learning algorithms,
employ interconnected nodes across various layers to process data,
emulating the human brain's intricate network of neurons and synapses.
While these algorithms have proven incredibly potent, they are not
universally the optimal solution for every problem. Their performance
can vary based on several factors, including the task's complexity, the
dataset's size, and the available computational resources. Despite these
considerations, ANNs have achieved remarkable success in a wide range of
applications, from image recognition (Quraishi, Choudhury, and De 2012),
fraud detection (Krambia-Kapardis, Christodoulou, and Agathocleous 2010;
Asha and KR 2021), medical diagnosis (Yen and Tsao 2024), and
classification of natural language patterns (Goldberg 2017), which is
our interest here.

## Fasttext

FastText (Bhattacharjee 2018) is a powerful library developed by
Facebook's AI Research team and designed for efficient text
classification and word embeddings (more about this topic in
Chapter [\[sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings\]](#sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings"}). In its
training process, it stands out by incorporating subword information,
such as character n-grams, alongside whole words. This approach enables
FastText to effectively handle texts with rare words or misspellings,
capturing their meanings based on subword patterns. In R, the
`fastTextR` package serves as an interface to this library, offering
functions for training text classification models, generating word
embeddings, and conducting nearest neighbor searches within the
embedding space. FastText's efficiency is particularly notable when
working with large datasets, allowing for rapid model training without
compromising accuracy. Another of FastText's key strengths is its
support for multiple languages, making it an excellent tool for
multilingual text analysis. FastText can be applied in various NLP
tasks, including sentiment analysis, where it helps identify emotional
tones in product reviews; language modeling, enhancing the performance
of predictive text systems; and named entity recognition, where it aids
in classifying names of people, organizations, and locations in
unstructured text, which is an exciting feature for any marketing
analyst looking for brands or brand names in text. In the following
chapters, we will tackle all these topics. Meanwhile, here is an example

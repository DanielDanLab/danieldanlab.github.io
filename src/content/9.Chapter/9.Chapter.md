Named Entity Recognition (NER) is used in NLP to find important
information in documents. The goal is to identify and classify basic
text elements and their interactions. Named Entities (NE) are important
to a specific purpose or topic, determining their type and boundary.
Although NEs do not have a direct and precise definition, maybe because
there are too many, they can be defined more from a practical point of
view. They are elements that describe categories, such as persons,
organizations, locations, dates, times, percentages, and monetary
amounts, among others. For example, a person can be identified by a name
or alias, and a location could be a city, village, or forest name. These
two categories, person and location, might be related by the place of
birth of someone identified by its name. NER is an NLP branch and aims
to help understand text questions such as Who? What? Where? When?
through simple elements (Nouvel, Ehrmann, and Rosset 2016).

As we already demonstrated in the previous chapter when tracking the
evolution of brand sentiment over time, in marketing, this information
can be used to monitor several aspects. A company might want to know the
opinion of the customers (who), what they think about their products or
brands (what), in which region or country (where), and at which point in
time (when.) They might also want to check competitors' technological
advancements, national or international marketing strategies, and pain
points of the brand's customer perceptions. Some reviewing platforms,
ranging in their focus from general topics (e.g., Yelp) to more
specialized areas, such as traveling (e.g., TripAdvisor) or movie review
platforms (e.g., IMDb), aim to collect information on products and link
them to consumer feedback (A.-M. Popescu and Etzioni 2007). If well
extracted, NEs can be considered the building blocks of a nice summary,
giving insight into the information contained in documents (Hassel
2003).

An important application of NER is the possibility of anonymizing the
personal data contained in a document; this is very important in several
fields since data privacy is paramount. Personal information can be
hidden while the related data and the possibility to interpret them can
be used for statistical purposes or the development of segment-specific
marketing campaigns.

## How does NER work?

There are several ways to implement NER, such as rule-based systems,
statistical models, and neural network models. Rule-based systems use
predefined lists of entities, for example, gazetteers[^1], and regular
expressions, like the ones we presented in
Chapter [\[sec-textual-data\]](#sec-textual-data){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-textual-data"}. This is relatively simple to implement,
but it is not very scalable or flexible if you have large heterogeneous
datasets. Statistical methods and machine learning algorithms use
methods, like those we discussed for the classification task in
Chapter [\[sec-classification\]](#sec-classification){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-classification"}. Some other frequently used approaches
employ Hidden Markov Models (HMM) (Morwal, Jahan, and Chopra 2012),
Conditional Random Fields (CRF) Sun et al. (2006), and Maximum Entropy
(ME) methods (Chieu and Ng 2003). These methods are more accurate than
the rule-based systems, but the data to be trained on must be of a
substantial size. The most accurate methods are the neural network
models. They use techniques such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) (Li
et al. 2015), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) (Zhu, Wang, and
Karlsson 2019), and Transformers (Arkhipov et al. 2019) to accomplish
NER tasks. The computational power required by such models is high, and
they also need large annotated datasets. Nevertheless, results are not
always perfect, and the process of identifying and classifying can be
faulty, especially with entities that can be ambiguous or
context-dependent. Take as an example Amazon, which can be related with
a forest (location) or a company (organization). Disambiguation is a
complex task, but most recent transformer technologies that understand
context well, have made this more and more precise. It is necessary to
mention that variability introduces misclassification, for example, in
cases where an entity is referred to in different ways, like CR7 or
Cristiano Ronaldo, this task is called coreference resolution, sometimes
called Named Entity Resolution, extremely useful in text analytics.

### Workflow

In an NER task, raw text must undergo several transformations, from a
preprocessing part to a final NER part. Here, we will explain a generic
NER method. The preprocessing task helps transform the text into a more
suitable format for subsequent NER procedures. It starts with segmenting
the text into sentences, which are represented by lists of strings; this
is followed by tokenization, which helps to break down text at a desired
granular level and becomes a list of strings. Next comes the the
Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging, which identifies which parts of speech
specific words belong to; tokens can be nouns, verbs, adjectives, and so
on, and can turn very useful for entity recognition. In modern contexts,
tokens can be transformed into word embeddings; we will discuss them in
Chapter [\[sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings\]](#sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings"}. Next is the
Entity detection part, which helps classify tokens into categories
through one of the models discussed. At this step, the boundaries of the
entity and its type are identified. Then, relation detection comes into
action with a final result in the form of text tuples associated with
entities and relationships. See
Figure [1.1](#fig-ner){reference-type="ref" reference="fig-ner"},
inspired by Dias et al. (2020) for a visual representation of the
process.

The landscape of linguistic annotations is made more complex by the use
of various formats, two of them being predominant: Conference of Natural
Language Learning Universal Dependencies (CoNNL-U; cf. Project (2022),
Schuster and Manning (2016)) and Text Interchange Format (TIF) (Mullen
et al. 2018). The former represents a highly standardized and widespread
format for dependency structures across various languages. Its form is a
plain text file containing several fields for each token, and it turns
useful when universal dependencies are needed. The latter, especially
used in R, offers a flexible structure for text package objects, such as
corpus and token objects. All R packages that support this standard will
be able to interact between them nicely; such R packages are `spacyr`
and `quanteda`.

The model of choice depends on several factors, for example, the
dimension of the dataset, the presence of labeled data, computational
capabilities, or the extreme need for precise accuracy of the results.

## NER using R

There are several packages that can be used in R. Each package might use
a different model to annotate the text. One of them is `nametagger`,
another is `OpenNLP`, which is based on the Apache OpenNLP library.
Related to the previous one is `entity`, which is a wrapper for `NLP`
and `OpenNLP`. Another is `crfsuite`, that provides an interface to CRF.
Yet, another one is `udpipe`, a package developed by the Belgium Network
of Open Source Analytical Consultants (BNOSAC). Packages like
`nametagger`, `crfsuite` or `udpipe` use CoNLL-U models [^2] while
`OpenNLP` has its own annotator. Last but not least, an important
package, `spacyr` (Benoit and Matsuo 2023), is used in R through and
interface to the Python `SpaCy` library (Vasiliev 2020). `SpaCy` uses
neural network models and tries to predict entities based on these
models, which are pre-trained on large datasets. Depending on the need
they are optimized for speed, sm (small), for a balance between speed
and accuracy, md (medium), and for higher accuracy but with more
computational power needed, lg (large). These models exist for several
languages, such as English, German, French, and many more. A pipeline
for how `Spacy` does annotations, is provided in
Figure [1.2](#fig-pipeline){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-pipeline"}. The first component, "tok2vec", transforms
tokens into vector embeddings (more about this in
Chapter [\[sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings\]](#sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings"}) that represent
semantic meanings in a numerical space. These vectors are used by both
the "tagger" which assigns POS tags, and the "parser" which analyzes the
grammatical structure of the sentence. The "attribute_ruler" applies
rules to attributes like lemma, POS, etc., and helps the "lemmatizer" to
standardize words to their root form. The last component, "ner",
identifies named entities. It might loop back, though, to "tok2vec" for
better detection.

The entities (or categories) for NER that `SpaCy` extracts are some
basic ones; some names are intuitive, and others might need an
explanation. The examples are not exhaustive.

-   PERSON: People, including fictional, aliases, former names;

-   NORP (Nationalities, or Religious or Political): Nationalities or
    religious or political groups;

-   FAC (Facility): Buildings, airports, highways, bridges, hospitals;

-   ORG: Companies, agencies, institutions, NGOs;

-   GPE (Geopolitical Entity): Countries, states, cities, rural areas;

-   LOC: Non-GPE locations, mountain ranges, bodies of water, deserts;

-   PRODUCT: Objects, vehicles, foods (Not services.);

-   EVENT: Named hurricanes, battles, wars, sports events, political
    events, cultural events, scientific events, economic events;

-   WORK_OF_ART: Titles of books, songs, paintings;

-   LAW: Named documents made into laws;

-   LANGUAGE: Any named language;

SpaCy includes some extended categories, too:

-   DATE: Absolute or relative dates or periods;

-   TIME: Times smaller than a day;

-   PERCENT: Percentage, including "

-   MONEY: Monetary values, including units;

-   QUANTITY: Measurements, as of weight or distance;

-   ORDINAL: "first", "second", etc.;

-   CARDINAL: Numerals, such as 1, 10, 100, three, seventeen;

The previous categories might have a letter assigned after them, for
example, DATE_B. This is because it follows the BILUO tagging scheme,
which stands for:

-   B: Beginning of an entity.

-   I: Inside of an entity (continuation of an entity started with B).

-   L: Last token of an entity.

-   U: Unit entity (a single-token entity).

-   O: Outside of any entity.

This is used when the entity spans multiple tokens, and the current
token is the first, inside, the last, etc. token in that sequence.

### Extracting Adjectives

The `pos` field identifies which category a word, or its lemma, belongs
to and uses a tag set that is generally well understood and useful
across various applications. For a more detailed treatment, especially
in English, `spaCy` utilizes the OntoNotes 5 version of the Penn
Treebank tag set, while other languages may use different Treebanks
[^3]. More details can be found in spaCy's documentation. With this
understanding of the text structure, we can now proceed to extract some
specific aspect of interest used in the analyzed content.

Emotions and sensory perceptions are expressed through adjectives and it
is useful to understand how they are used because they help to
differentiate a brand and to detect underlying pain points. Repeated use
of adjectives in reviews or their appearance in slogans (Ke and Wang
2013) might persuade (Ebaid 2018) the audience about a product or a
service and emphasize strengths or weaknesses. Adjectives are commonly
used in commerce, whether they appear in simple basic form, comparative,
or superlative (Hakkarainen 2016). They can elicit emotions and make
that a specific attribute of a brand to be remembered Berger, Kim, and
Meyer (2021). We select the ADJ part of the reviews, group by adjective
token and brand, count the occurrences, filter adjectives with more than
one occurrence, and sort by descending counts. After this, we list the
first 20 most frequent adjectives and the brands they are related to.

### Dependencies and POS highlighting

It is interesting and useful to obtain additional information from the
text and better understand how parts of speech contribute to meaning.
`spacyr` in conjunction with the package `rsyntax` help plot a tree of
POS, highlighting the dependencies between words. If you want another
example, you can substitute "text1" and assign to "doc_id" a document of
your choice, see Figure [1.3](#fig-ner7){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-ner7"} to visualize a syntactic dependency parse tree and
understand the grammatical structure of a sentence. Words are related to
one another and this is indicated by the branches. The arrows points
from a head word to a dependent word. The numbers correspond to the word
order in a sentence to keep track of the order of appearance. The ROOT
of the tree is the main verb, represents the central action while other
words are linked through different dependency relations. There are
several labels in the image, such as "nsubjh", "dobj", "punct", they
come from the Universal Dependencies (UD) annotation scheme. For
example, "nobj" represents a nominal subject (who or what performs an
action), "dobj" represents a direct object (the one that receives the
action of a verb), "punct" is a punctuation mark. For a more complete
definition of all the labels, see (De Marneffe et al. 2021) . This type
of analysis is also suitable for chatbots, search engines, or grammar
correction tools.

### Language complexity

Sometimes researchers are interested in studying the syntactic
complexity in the relationship with the language proficiency reflected
in texts and the overall (or "objective") proficiency in a target
language (Ortega 2003). When conducting these measurements, one must
compare them against a benchmark or another unit, for example, unit A
compared to unit B on a specific index can provide insights into
accuracy and applicability (Szmrecsanyi 2004). There are several metrics
used to calculate syntactic complexity, and they are mainly used in
linguistics, language acquisition, and computational linguistics. In
this subchapter, we demonstrate the use of a simple metric, which is
based on the measurement of the density of verbs called Fichtner's C
(Fichtner 1980; Samples, Ireland, and Kraczon 2024), which is defined
as:

$$\text{Fichtner's C} = \frac{\text{Number of Verbs}}{\text{Number of Sentences}} \times \frac{\text{Number of Words}}{\text{Number of Sentences}}$$

Using syntactic complexity in marketing allows a detailed comparison of
the language used to describe different brands. By analyzing metrics
like Fichtner's C, marketers can gain insights into how brands are
perceived and what level of complexity is used to describe them, thus
tailoring their communication style to a specific audience. The code
below computes Fichtner's C index and compares linguistic complexity
between brands displaying a table.

### A final technical note

Several advanced techniques can be employed to enhance the capabilities
of an NER task. An example can be the use of consolidation functions to
merge multi-word entities into a single token or the concatenation of
words into noun phrases, also called noun chunks. `SpaCy` can use
language models that are not limited only to English but also include
German, Spanish, Portugues, French, Italian, and Dutch, among others.
`spacyr` can be integrated with other R text analysis packages, for
example, `quanteda`, or `tidytext`, thus simplifying text manipulation
and analysis tasks.

Additionally, as we have seen in
Chapter [\[sec-textual-data\]](#sec-textual-data){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-textual-data"}, regular expressions can be helpful in
extracting preposition-noun combinations from the corpus and checking
the most frequent noun for each preposition or many other parts of
speech. A clear understanding of the annotations and the meanings of the
tags at different levels, as well as tag sets present in various
languages, is essential because these could influence the approach to
NER and text analysis in general.

## Summarization

Talking about POS, the word summary relates to something that is brief
and concise. The word comes from the Latin word "summa," which means
"totality, gist. One will find summarizations everywhere, such as the
summary of a book, at the workplace, in the meeting minutes, in news
articles, and in customer reviews. Because we focus on the latter one,
we must understand that this allows for an aggregated view of opinions,
delivering something similar to"the wisdom of the crowd ."Many texts are
too long to read, while we have too little time, often encountered as
"too long, did not read." The abbreviation of this is TL;DR (again a
shorter version of something), which is commonly used in online
discussion, social media, etc., indicates content that is lengthy and a
summary is provided for whoever does not have time to read the whole
text. TL;DR can be used as a label for the summary itself or as a
comment, to indicate that the text is too long. Summaries are also
useful for information retention to enhance the ability to learn
something (Reder and Anderson 1980), and it implies conveying
information in a very abridged form, creating a shortened version that
keeps the original content's essential information and main points. But
how much can one summarize? In NLP, summarization can be achieved mainly
using three approaches: extractive (Moratanch and Chitrakala 2017),
abstractive (Som Gupta and Gupta 2019), or hybrid (El-Kassas et al.
2021). The first approach, uses a selection (and extraction) of key
sentences, or phrases, from the original text that act as a summary.
This selection is typically based on some statistical features, such as
importance scores, term frequency, or position. The second one, is a
generative process that uses rephrasing or paraphrasing with words that
might not even exist in the original text. This last approach involves
understanding the context and producing an abridged version that
captures the gist of the original text. The hybrid method is a
combination of the first two. Below we will focus on the extractive
summarization because it can be accomplished relatively quick, although
it might be computationally intensive. It also fits nicely with the NER
part we have discussed so far.

### Summarization in R

There are a few packages that help with the task of summarization in R,
either directly or indirectly. One is `lexRankr`, another one is LSAfun,
and yet, another one is `textrank`. The simplest one to work with is
`lexRankr`. The implementation of the algorithm in R uses a stochastic
graph-based method to assess the importance of a text unit, such as a
sentence, in a corpus (Erkan and Radev 2004). The basic idea is that, if
the algorithm finds sentences that are similar to other sentences, it
means that they are important. In this graph-base approach, sentences
are nodes, edges represent the similarity between sentences. To
illustrate, let us take the "data" dataframe and apply the algorithm.

Another graph-based ranking method (Mihalcea and Tarau 2004) to extract
sentences or keywords is `textrank` (Wijffels 2020). The nodes of the
graph represent a sentence, and the edges between them are computations
of the number of words adjacent nodes have in common. This structure is
passed on to the PageRank algorithm (Xing and Ghorbani 2004),
(implemented in Google's search engine (Brin and Page 1998) and named
after one of its founders, Larry Page,) to find the most important
sentences that will form the summary of the text. In other words, the
summary is given by the most important sentences. A summary can also be
provided by using keywords. They represent documents and have the
capability to distill the content and express concepts, themes, or
topics in just a few words, making the process of information indexing,
search, and retrieval easier. In the `textranking` algorithm, a word
network is built, similar to those that you have already seen in,
Chapter [\[sec-sentiment-analysis\]](#sec-sentiment-analysis){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-sentiment-analysis"},
Section [\[sec-sentiment-word-networks\]](#sec-sentiment-word-networks){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-sentiment-word-networks"}, for example. The link between
words becomes more important as two words frequently follow one another.
In this case, too, PageRank is applied, and the top $\frac{1}{3}$ of the
found keywords are considered important. Finding which words are
relevant can be extracted through POS tagging that we have done in the
NER part of this chapter with `spacyr`. Let us set up the scene and
extract the important keywords. We create a table that contains the

### A summarization note

Abstractive summarization is a more complex form of text summarization
compared to extractive summarization. It involves generating new
sentences that convey the main ideas of the original text. In R,
abstractive summarization is not as straightforward as extractive
summarization due to the lack of direct support for complex neural
network models within R itself. However, you can use R to interface with
other tools or libraries that support abstractive summarization, such as
Python libraries via reticulate or external APIs. But in the next
chapter, we discuss more advanced tools to accomplish several text
analysis tasks, and this will allow us to generate text, too.

## Text manipulation

### Introduction

It is rare for a marketing analyst to work with perfectly crafted texts.
Since the focus of this book is on analyzing user-generated content
(UGC), such as online reviews of products or services, blog entries, or
social media posts, from a marketing perspective, we cannot expect any
of these texts to be free from linguistic errors, typos, or
misspellings. Unless they are revised or edited by professionals, these
texts are likely to be imperfect (Moens, Li, and Chua 2014).

Such linguistic errors in UCG can impact the analyses of these texts
and, in turn, the marketing implications derived from them negatively.
For instance, typos in reviews can shift the overall sentiment. Mistakes
can lead to the misinterpretation of texts and potentially omit
essential product information. Different spellings of the product name
or features can make product comparisons inconsistent. Incorrectly
spelled keywords might make it challenging for companies to track
conversations and analyze customer sentiment (Berger et al. 2020). In
some contexts, misspelling can even be used as an additional measure to
reveal information about the communicator (Netzer, Lemaire, and
Herzenstein 2019). These are not the only problems found in UGC. There
are also punctuation errors, incorrect capitalization, nonstandard
abbreviations and acronyms, misused emojis, homophones, slang, jargon,
incomplete sentences, and many more elements which can be
misinterpreted. All in all, there are countless ways to write a text
which present problems for textual analysis.

Thus, we often encounter situations when we need to search for, select,
and partly modify or transform words, numbers, or whole phrases. This
process, known as text manipulation, requires specific skills. It is
necessary when aggregating text from different sources, such as from
online product reviews with authors from different backgrounds,
cultures, or with varying styles. The main goal of altering text in
reviews is to create documents that are error-free, uniform, and ready
to be analyzed. This is important because humans can correct misspelled
words mentally but are slow, computers cannot make such a distinction
but are fast. If the text is not polished, the resulting statistics will
be flawed: for example, different spellings of the same word will be
treated as separate words by the computer.

To catch common spelling errors, most of us use a spell checker of some
form when we compose a text document, or at least we should have it
activated. A spell checker is a function built into common text writing
programs that detect misspellings by comparing the words in a drafted
document with a database containing standard spellings. Among others, a
popular text-mining package that includes prepackaged spellers to
correct mistakes is the Enchant speller (AbiWord Project 2024). Modern
applications use artificial intelligence algorithms to do this, but
although using such automatic tools for large-scale correction may seem
convenient, it comes with risks. The main challenge is that these tools
may only sometimes determine context. This can lead to incorrect changes
and generate unwanted errors. With advancements in AI, though,
contextual understanding is becoming more accurate, thereby reducing
such risks (Hu et al. 2020).

The discrepancy between English language variants that have a disparity
in standardization, such as British English (GB) or American English
(US) spelling, is another problem (Hitchings 2008). When dealing with
aggregated data, the probability of encountering both in the same corpus
is high. Thus, a method to make them uniform is desirable. We can create
ad-hoc functions or procedures to manipulate text to overcome these
difficulties. The operations we employ to achieve take three forms:
additive, subtractive, and substitutive. In the first case, we can add
characters, strings, or numbers to a specific string anywhere, such as
at the beginning, in the middle or at the end of a word, and before or
after a specific character. In the same way, we can subtract or
substitute them to obtain the desired result. In essence, **text is your
data**, and managing it effectively is essential for accurate analysis.

Now we will briefly introduce some basic concepts of how (textual) data
are stored inside R. Readers who are merely interested in applications
can skip this section. Later, we will delve into more advanced
techniques and functions to help us manipulate textual data effectively.

### Some basics on measurement representation in R {#some-basics-on-measurement-representations-in-r}

Data is more than knowledge (Tuomi 1999). The Oxford dictionary defines
**data** (Coombs 1964) as: "The quantities, characters, or symbols on
which operations are performed by a computer, which may be stored and
transmitted in the form of electrical signals and recorded on magnetic,
optical, or mechanical recording media." It is easy to see the
**quantities** as numbers, the **characters** as letters, and the
**symbols** as images, and consider all these as **types of data**. In
computer programming, we deal with two fundamental entities for
accessing stored data: **variables** and **constants** (Ihaka and
Gentleman 1996). Variables in programming are used to point to stored
data by assigning them an understandable, meaningful, and concise name.
For example, we could use the variable name `temperature` to refer to a
sequence of temperature values that you have measured. Then, the
variable can be referenced and modified as needed by other computer
routines. The **constant** represents a fixed value that cannot be
altered but can be used in other functions. A classic example is **pi**,
the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter, commonly
rounded down to **3.14**. Data need to be structured (Arasu and
Garcia-Molina 2003) to facilitate easy manipulation. For the sake of
precision, you need to understand that a **variable** represents a
reference to a structure which has been stored; it does not store the
data, it just gives them a name.

#### Vectors

A **vector** is the simplest form of data structure in R, commonly used
alongside lists, matrices, arrays, and data frames. A vector can be
**atomic**, meaning that it contains elements that are all of the same
data type, for example logical, integer, double, or character data. It
can contain a single value, or it can hold multiple values. In
Figure [1.1](#fig-array){reference-type="ref" reference="fig-array"}, we
can make an analogy between a vector, which contains variables of a
single data type, and a single-column chest of drawers filled with
socks. Each drawer represents an element in the vector, and we have
named this vector `drawers`.

To populate our vector, we assign values to it using the variable name
(`drawers`) and a discrete number of differently colored socks: **23
red**, **32 blue**, **15 green**, **41 yellow**, and **12 white**. We
use the function, `c()`, which stands for **combine**, to group these
"socks" together. The assignment is done using the `<-` operator:
`drawers <- c(23, 32, 15, 41, 12)`.

To access the content of any drawer, we simply use the variable name and
specify the desired element(s) using **square brackets**. For instance,
`drawers``[``3``]` retrieves the content of the third element of the
vector variable, which is **15 green socks**. Remember, what appears in
the R console are numbers, not actual socks. If we want to know the
contents of the second and fifth drawers, we type:
`drawers``[``c(2, 5)``]`, which will return the number of socks in both
the second and fifth elements of the vector. As you can see, our vector
contains **only socks**, not shirts or pants. This shows that a vector
can contain only elements of the same type (e.g., all numbers, all
integers, or all characters). On top of that, it is **one-dimensional**,
because it is flat and linear.

#### Arrays

Let us introduce another piece of terminology: the **array**. In R, an
**array** is like a vector but can have more than one dimension. An
example of a two-dimensional array is a **matrix**. Technically, they
are the same thing, but R likes to keep these two concepts separate due
to the significant role matrices play in statistical computation.
Obviously, we can have arrays with more than two dimensions, but
visualizing these higher-dimensional structures can be challenging, if
not impossible.

In R, a **list** is a data structure that can store elements of varying
types---such as numbers, strings, **vectors**, or even other lists. The
key difference between a **list** and a **vector** lies in the types of
data they can contain. While a **vector** must consist of elements of
the **same data type**, a list can hold **heterogeneous data types**,
including vectors, other lists, and even lists of lists. This
flexibility makes lists a versatile data structure in R.

#### Vector Types in R

R language categorizes data into six basic atomic vector types: three
numerical types (integer, numeric, complex), a logical type (true or
false), a character type, and a raw type. For a more comprehensive
understanding of these types, refer to the works of Kabacoff (2022) and
Wickham (2019), which provide detailed explanations and examples.

These types are named intuitively to reflect the kind of data they hold.
For instance, **integer vectors** store whole numbers, **numeric
vectors** can hold decimal numbers, **complex vectors** are for complex
numbers, **logical vectors** contain Boolean values (TRUE or FALSE),
**character vectors** store text, and **raw vectors** are used for raw
bytes. As an analogy, think of the data type as the material from which
each chest of drawers is made. For instance, some might be made of wood
(representing integers), others of plastic (representing characters),
and some others of metal (representing logical values like TRUE or
FALSE).

#### Data Frames

A **data frame** in R is structured like a table, and is similar to a
table in many standard spreadsheet programs such as Microsoft Excel,
Google Sheets, Apple Numbers, or LibreOffice Calc.

Just as a table in these programs is composed of rows and columns, an R
**data frame** is structured in the same way. Each **column** in a data
frame can be considered to be a vector, holding data of the same type,
much like a spreadsheet column containing numbers, text, or dates. The
**rows** in a data frame correspond to individual records or
observations, similar to how each row in a spreadsheet represents a
specific item, person, or event.

To continue with our analogy between a data frame in R and a chest of
drawers in the physical world, let us consider each row in the data
frame to represent a person, see
Figure [1.2](#fig-dataframe){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-dataframe"}. In technical terms, the data in each **row**
is called an **observation**. A **column** represents a specific
feature, called an **attribute**. Each person, or row, is linked to
various features, which in our analogy are items like socks, t-shirts,
and pants. For instance, Aida might have 23 socks, 16 t-shirts, and 8
pairs of pants.

#### Manipulating Data Frames

In this context, the chest of drawers can be thought of as our data
frame in R. Each drawer or column represents a different item of
clothing. We access these items in R in a similar way to opening a
specific drawer of the piece of furniture. For example, if we want to
check how many t-shirts Carl has, and Carl is the third person in our
data frame, we would use the command `chest``[``3, 2``]` in R. This
command is akin to opening the second drawer (t-shirts) of the third row
(Carl) of our chest of drawers.

We can also 'slice' this data frame, which is just like selecting
specific drawers from our chest of drawers. If we want to see only the
t-shirts and pants for everyone, we use `chest``[``, c(2, 3)``]`. The
empty space before the comma indicates that we are selecting all the
rows or, in our analogy, all the people. After the comma, the `c(2, 3)`
specifies that we only want to look at the second and third columns,
which are t-shirts and pants.

In this analogy, instead of actual socks, t-shirts, and pants, we are
dealing with different types of data in our data frame. So, where you
might imagine long socks in a drawer, in R, this could correspond to a
column of strings or numbers representing that particular item or
attribute.

Imagine that your chest of drawers can be part of different sets of
furniture. One chest might belong to a set for a bedroom (similar to a
data frame **class**), another might be part of a set of office
furniture (similar to a list **class**), and so on. The set of furniture
(**class**) it belongs to determines how the chest of drawers is used. A
bedroom chest of drawers might be used to store clothes, while an office
version might be organized to store files and documents. In R, the
**class** of an object determines how it behaves with different
functions and operations. For example, functions treat a data frame (a
bedroom set) differently than a basic vector (a standalone chest of
drawers). The difference between **data type** and **class** is the
following. The first refers to the **low-level internal structure** of
how R stores data in memory. A variable can be checked for its type
using the `typeof()` function. while the second is a **high-level**
**abstraction** that focuses on how an object behaves in R. Because the
word **class** is related to object oriented programming, it defines how
functions and methods interact with an object. As a reminder, an
**object** is an element which stores information, it is referenced by a
name, which is the name of the variable.

#### Empty Strings and Character Vectors

Just as we use a drawer to store garments, we can use specific
structures in programming to store text strings. Let us imagine we have
a chest of drawers, but we will leave these drawers empty. In the
context of programming, particularly in R, text strings are categorized
as belonging to a **character class**. So we use another data type now,
a **character**, not an integer.

Let us consider an **empty string**, which is similar to having a chest
of drawers with only one column of drawers, and those drawers are empty.
We can assign an **empty string** to a variable; let us call it
`empty_string`. This would be shown in R as `empty_string <- ""`,
signifying a string that contains no characters. Paradoxically, even
though the string is empty, it belongs to the **character class** in R.

When we are uncertain about the class type of a variable, we can easily
check it using the `class()` function. For instance, typing
`class(empty_string)` would confirm that empty_string is indeed a
**character class** despite being empty. This is similar to confirming
that an empty drawer is still a part of the chest of drawers, designed
to hold items even when it currently does not.

#### Combining and Indexing Strings

through indexing, which allows us to access and modify elements at
particular locations in the vector. For example, if we want to place the
word 'five' in the fifth position of our vector, we use indexing. This
is done by adding square brackets `[``]` after the vector's name,
`character_vector`, and specifying the position we want with a number.
In this case, we use the number 5 to indicate the fifth position. The
corresponding R code would be `character_vector``[``5``]`` <- five,`

### Regular expressions in R

The previous sections served as a warm-up, using R-based procedures to
introduce you to the fascinating world of text analytics. Next, we will
explore the basics of regular expressions, a powerful tool for
searching, parsing, and manipulating text. All the examples in this
section are designed to work with the base R functions, meaning you do
not need to install any extra packages.

Regular expressions, often abbreviated as regex or regexp, are used not
only in search engines and by word processors, but are also integral to
many programming languages for detecting, locating, or replacing
information. This section will illustrate the basic concept and some
elementary functions of regular expressions in the R computing
environment (Sanchez 2013). For a more detailed exploration, see Friedl
(2006) or Forta (2018).

R supports two types of regular expression paradigms: POSIX and
Perl-like. The POSIX standard, an acronym for 'Portable Operating System
Interface,' comprises a set of IEEE (Institute of Electrical and
Electronics Engineers) standards. These standards aim to provide a
consistent and portable interface for programming and user environments
across various operating systems, including but not limited to
Unix-based systems. The POSIX standard has two types of regular
expressions: basic regular expressions (BRE) and extended regular
expressions (ERE). R uses ERE by default in its regular expression
functions, such as `grep`, `grepl`, `sub`, and `gsub`.

Perl had a large community that has significantly contributed to the
development of a rich set of regex libraries and documentation, and it
was renowned for its early and robust integration of regular
expressions. This makes Perl's regex particularly well-suited for
complex pattern-matching tasks. In R, Perl-compatible regular
expressions can be used by setting the perl = TRUE option, which allows
for the use of advanced regex features within R's regex functions.

For further reading and a deeper understanding of these topics, see the
the following: Lewine (1991); Cicekli et al. (2010); and Gagolewski
(2022).

### The foundations of regular expressions {#foundations-of-regular-expressions}

A regular expression is a sequence of characters and metacharacters
which together form a search pattern. For instance, a simple pattern

represents a concise string that tests for a specific pattern capable of
extracting or replacing substrings. Regular expressions can search for a
variety of elements, including letters (e.g., 'a', 'b', 'c'), numbers
(e.g., '0', '1', '5'), or other characters (e.g., '=', '-'). To the
untrained eye, these patterns might look as random as the keystrokes of

#### Position of a pattern inside a string {#position-of-pattern-inside-a-string}

In the following section, we will focus on anchors and boundaries in
regular expressions, as detailed in sources like Bryan (2020). These
elements are crucial for specifying the position of a string within a
sequence of characters. Anchors denote a string's start and end, such as
the circumflex (\^) for the former and the dollar sign (\$) for the
latter. Boundaries, on the other hand, define specific positions within
the string. A summary of these anchors and boundaries, along with their
descriptions, is provided in

  ------------------------ -----------------------------------

We use an anchor in regular expressions to locate strings which start or
end with a specific word or character. Conversely, a boundary is used to
find a position between a word character (like letters and numbers) and
a non-word character (like spaces or punctuation). While the beginning
or end of a string could be considered a boundary, in regular
expressions, anchors and boundaries are distinct concepts with specific
uses. For example, if we want to capture all the sentences which start
with the character sequence 'Ki', we would use the `^` anchor followed
by 'Ki'. The `^` anchor explicitly matches a string's start, so `^Ki`
would find any string that begins with 'Ki'.

#### Regular expressions with the stringr package

The `stringr` package, part of Hadley Wickham's `tidyverse` suite of
tools, can be installed independently or accessed as part of the entire
`tidyverse` collection (refer to the introduction for installation
details; Wickham 2022b). All the main functions in `stringr` start with
the prefix `str_` and are designed for efficient string manipulation and
the application of regular expressions. One key advantage of using
`stringr` is its standardization across the two regex standards
implemented in R, which means you do not need to specify
`perl=TRUE/FALSE` for regex operations (Bryan 2020). Examples of such
functions are: `str_detect()` for finding patterns, `str_subset()` for
extracting matching elements, and `str_split_fixed()` for splitting
strings based on patterns. It is worth mentioning that the function
`str_view()` checks the representation of a string and how a pattern
matches.

#### Regular expressions in search, and search and replace within RStudio {#regular-expressions-in-search-search-and-replace-within-rstudio}

RStudio helps in using regular expression patterns to search for or
replace text in R, Rnw, Rmd, or other types of file. To practice your
regex skills in RStudio, you can create a regex sequence and then enable
the 'Regex' and 'Wrap' options. For instance, consider the string "Text
Analytics for Digital Marketing". Using the capturing parentheses
expression `(Text)(.*?)(i)` in the search box will match the sequence
"Text Analyti". This pattern uses three groups. The first one captures
"Text", the second captures any examples of no character or least
possible number, and the third captures "i" at its first occurrence (the
least possible). Similarly `(ext)(.*?)(i)`, will match "ext Analyti".
The addition of the middle group (`.*?)` makes the this 'lazy', meaning
it matches the shortest possible sequence available rather than the
longest. The main difference between greedy (standard in R) and lazy
(enabled in RStudio) matching, is that the former aims to match the
longest possible sequence, while the latter matches the shortest. The
(`?`) is the only way to instruct the regex engine to stop at the
shortest possible match for a specified quantifier. For substitution,
you can replace the content of one of the groups. For example, to change
"Text Analytics for Digital Marketing" to "My Text Analytics for Digital
Marketing":

-   In the search box, enter `(Text)(.*)(Marketing)`

-   In the replace box, enter `My Text\2\3`

This means that the second and third groups remain unchanged, and only
"Text" is replaced with "My text". While R's regex engines (POSIX and
Perl-like) do not inherently support lazy matching, RStudio, as an
integrated development environment, provides this functionality,
demonstrating the flexibility different tools offer in handling regular
expressions. As a short exercise using the lazy version, you can try to
substitute a string inside a sentence.

## Grouping text and linguistic elements

Humans tend to categorize (Harnad 2017): This is a cognitive shortcut
which identifies commonalities and creates groups, enabling the
efficient processing of information. This is part of human cognition and
allows us to act in a predetermined way when a specific, already
familiar, situation arises. It is also serves as a method of evaluating
homogeneity in data so that we can make decisions based on a restricted
number of groups with specific characteristics. It is similar to
summarizing: We condense a lot of information into few important key
points that describe the whole in a concise manner.

One practical application of categorizing is clustering. This involves
identifying shared characteristics within data and grouping similar
items accordingly. There are several clustering techniques, as well as
many fields of application. In marketing, clustering can also be
referred to as segmentation, i.e., when we try to find a segment of a
population that exhibits similar behaviors and patterns. Of clustering
methods one is model-based and uses statistical probability models,
another is density-based clustering, which uses a model that groups
objects based on objective functions, while yet another is
distance-based and has a strong exploratory component. Various measures
can be used for this last version, including Hamming for categorial
data, Manhattan and Euclidean for continuous data, Jaccard for binary
data, Mahalanobis for correlated data, or cosine similarity for text
data. There are also hybrid methods which combine various clustering
methods (Vries, Olech, and Moscato 2019).

A similar concept to clustering is classification. This uses existing
classes with known features: New unclassified elements are tested
against these features and then assigned to the appropriate class, so
classification is a supervised method (Sen, Hajra, and Ghosh 2020).
Clustering seeks to uncover these commonalities autonomously, often
through iterative, unsupervised mathematical operations within
algorithms. Often we do not know how many clusters we can have and need
to conduct several trials before we can extract meaningful insights into
the data. This is a brief outline of two important concepts, clustering
and classification, and in this chapter we will concentrate on the
principles and application of the former.

In clustering, the goal is to extract latent groups, or clusters, that
contain a certain number of elements. These clusters may range from
those which contain a single element to those in which all elements
within a dataset are present. In any clustering algorithm the concept of
similarity is twofold: high similarity (homogeneity) within clusters and
marked dissimilarity (heterogeneity) between different clusters, as
illustrated in Figure [1.1](#fig-clusteringin){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-clusteringin"}. When applying a clustering algorithm,
several factors are of interest, including the number of clusters
generated, their relative distances, sizes, shapes, and densities.

Text clustering gives us a quick overview of the content of a corpus by
extracting its most representative features. Grouping together the words
that are most closely related to each other (Struhl 2015) helps us to
identify underlying topics and themes. These methods can be very useful,
for example, when clustering search results or finding similar word
groups in emails from customers. Such insights are essential in refining
segmentation strategies and discovering recurring issues in customer
communications, and so helping to develop one-to-one marketing.

## Proximity and similarity

### Euclidean distance {#sec-euclidean-distance}

In text mining, clustering can be applied at various levels, for entire
books, chapters, paragraphs, sentences, or even individual words.
Clustering words means grouping terms that appear at similar frequencies
across different documents (see the definition of document in the
introductory chapter), while clustering documents allows us to
understand the similarities between them. Authorship attribution is one
practical application, where texts are grouped to assign them to
specific authors. Each author's unique writing style, manifested through
their use of stop words, distinctive expressions, word frequencies, and
punctuation, becomes an identifier for such analyses (Stamatatos et al.
2016). Research has also shown gender-based differences in writing
styles, indicating potential gender segmentation in texts (Cheng,
Chandramouli, and Subbalakshmi 2011).

Frequency (i.e., how many times a term appears in a text) allows us to
use algorithms that involve the concept of Euclidean distance (Huang et
al. 2008). Documents with a high degree of similarity of word
frequencies are considered closer in terms of Euclidean distance,
meaning that the distance between them is minimal. A simple example will
help to grasp the basic concept.

Consider an unlikely scenario of three documents, each containing just
two words. Each word's occurrence in a document is quantified by its
frequency, expressed as the number of times it appears. To demonstrate
this concept, we will use R to replicate the data presented in
Table [1.1](#tbl-firsttable){reference-type="ref"

example is shown.

After we bind the documents and the words together in a tabular
structure, the data frame, we can calculate the distance between the
documents. Note that the concept of distance is associated with the
concept of similarity: The smaller the distance, the higher the
similarity. Figure [1.2](#fig-euclideandist){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-euclideandist"} gives a visual interpretation of this
concept, illustrating that `doc1` and `doc2` are closer to one another
when compared with `doc3`. Mathematically, this can be calculated using
Euclidean distance (Jockers and Thalken 2020b). On a two-dimensional
plane, this is analogous to the hypotenuse in the theorem of Pythagoras.
Be aware that Eucledian distance is not the only method that can be used
to calculate the distance between two or more elements. The work of Deza
and Deza (2009) offers an extensive overview of distance metrics
covering a wide range of calculation methods.

The equation to calculate the distance between `doc1` and `doc2` uses
$w$ to represent word frequencies in the documents. It is essential to
understand that $1^{st}w_{doc_1}$, $1^{st}w_{doc_2}$, etc., represent
the frequencies of the first words in **doc1** and **doc2**
respectively. The pattern continues for all words, up the $n^{th}$ word.
This is a simplification which assumes that both documents contain
exactly the same set of words. For words that are present in one
document and absent in the other, their frequency in the document where
they are absent is considered to be zero. The equation calculates the
Euclidean distance by summing the squares of differences in word
frequencies for each word across the two documents and then taking the
square root of this sum.
$$dist(doc_1, doc_2)=\sqrt{(1^{st}w_{doc_1}-1^{st}w_{doc_2})^2+(2^{nd}w_{doc_1}-2^{nd}w_{doc_2})^2+\ldots+(n^{th}w_{doc_1}-n^{th}w_{doc_2})^2}$$

In our case, we do not have *n* words, but only two: *bank* and
*engine*. Let us calculate the distance between the documents and round
the result to the first two decimals.

$$dist(doc_1, doc_2)=\sqrt{(20-22)^2+(11-12)^2}=2.24$$
$$dist(doc_1, doc_3)=\sqrt{(20-12)^2+(11-27)^2}=17.89$$
$$dist(doc_2, doc_3)=\sqrt{(22-12)^2+(12-27)^2}=18.03$$

As was expected from the scatter plot, the Eucledian distance between
documents $d_1$ and $d_2$ is considerably smaller than the distance
between $d_1$ and $d_3$, or between $d_2$ and $d_3$. To facilitate the
calculation of these distances, especially in cases involving many
documents with multiple features, we can use the R function `dist()`.
This efficiently calculates the distances of pairs between the
documents.

Euclidean distance is a simple method for calculating proximity but in
marketing it is not frequently applied due to its sensitivity to the
magnitude of the term frequencies. Another metric, called cosine
distance is preferred, as it measures the angle between vectors, we will
discuss about it more in detail in
Chapter [\[sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings\]](#sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-extracting-information-from-embeddings"}. In marketing
applications, cosine distance helps analyze text similarity, such as
clustering customer reviews or segmenting product descriptions (Berger
et al. 2020).

### Cosine similarity

We can calculate the cosine similarity of two vectors by considering the
data from the previous subchapter. We can consider a document expressed
as a vector, the elements of the vector are the frequencies of the words
in a document (J. Han, Pei, and Tong 2022). The cosine similarity
between two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ is defined as:

$$\phantomsection{
\text{sim}(\vec{A}, \vec{B}) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n A_i \cdot B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n A_i^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n B_i^2}}
}$$

In
Equation [\[eq-cosine_similarity\]](#eq-cosine_similarity){reference-type="ref"
reference="eq-cosine_similarity"}, $A_i$ and $B_i$ are the $i\text{-th}$
components, in our case word frequencies, of the two vectors. The
numerator represents their dot product while the denominator is the
product of their Euclidean magnitudes. We have the three documents
represented as vectors, $\vec{doc_1} = (20, 11)$,
$\vec{doc_2} = (22, 12)$, $\vec{doc_2} = (12, 27)$. The cosine
similarity between $\vec{doc_1}$ and $\vec{doc_2}$ is calculated like

$$\text{sim}(\vec{doc_1}, \vec{doc_2}) = \frac{(20 \cdot 22) + (11 \cdot 12)}{\sqrt{20^2 + 11^2} \cdot \sqrt{22^2 + 12^2}}
= \frac{572}{22.83 \cdot 25.07} \approx 0.998$$ Similarly we can
calculate the cosine similarity between $\vec{doc_1}$ and $\vec{doc_3}$,
and between $\vec{doc_2}$ and $\vec{doc_2}$. The closer the similarity
measure between two vectors is to $1$, the more similar they are. In R
we can use the package `lsa` to speed up the computational part.

## Hyerarchical Agglomerative Clustering

If we do not want to cluster or find document similarities but we need
to focus more on the words, a directly related application, exemplifying
the principles discussed in
Section [1.2.1](#sec-euclidean-distance){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec-euclidean-distance"}, is Hierarchical Clustering (HC),
one of the simplest forms of clustering. HC has two primary
methodologies: agglomerative and divisive. In agglomerative (or
bottom-up) clustering, each element initially represents a distinct
cluster. Then, they are merged or "agglomerated" into increasingly
larger clusters culminating in a single, comprehensive cluster. In the
divisive (or top-down) clustering the algorithm begins with one big
cluster that is sistematically divided into smaller and smaller ones
ultimately reahcing the level of individual elements in a tree-like
taxonomy. The analogy with a tree (dendrogram) is straightforward: the
leaves symbolize individual objects, nodes signify clusters and the root
contains the entire dataset, as illustrated in
Figure [1.3](#fig-clusteringhac){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-clusteringhac"}.

Eucledian distance. But a cluster contains one or more than one element.
So, if the distance between individual elements is well understood, how
would we calculate the distance between clusters? There ar several ways
to do that. The distance between two clusters can be given by the
distance between the closest elements contained in the two clusters. Or
the distance between the furthest elements. Or the mean distance between
all pairs of elements, one from each cluster. Each of these methods,
known collectively as linkage criteria, influences the shape and size of
the clusters formed. To better visualize these concepts,

We notice that the diagonal of the distance matrix is always zero
because the distance between a word and itself is null. In symmetric
distance matrices the values above and below the diagonal mirror each
other, hence only one part, the lower one, is usually displayed for
simplicity. For example, the distance between `Word1` and `Word3` will
be the same distance as the one between `Word3` and `Word1`. Using the
Euclidean distance formula, we calculate these distances and then apply
single-linkage clustering, also known as the minimum method. In other
words, we search for the minimum value between two elements. Initially,
all the elements form a cluster on their own, resulting in six separate
clusters. The minimum value between two values is between `Word3` and
`Word6`, as highlighted in Table [1.3](#tbl-dd1){reference-type="ref"
reference="tbl-dd1"}, leading to their combination into the first
cluster. There are two vertical lines that represent the individual
clusters that merge into a single line that represents the new cluster.
The number 1 in Figure [1.5](#fig-hac1){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-hac1"} states that this is the first cluster, the
vertical line indicates at what height on the dendrogram the cluster was
produced, in this case at **10.20**. The "height" represents the
distance or dissimilarity between clusters. If two clusters join at a
low height, it suggests they are quite similar or close to each other.
Conversely, if two clusters join at a higher height, it indicates a
larger dissimilarity or distance, suggesting that the clusters are less

The determination of the optimal number of clusters in a dataset is
often challenging, as there is no definitive answer. Several heuristic
methods, commonly referred to as stopping rules, may offer guidance by
generating a score or index, guiding the decision on the ideal number of
clusters. The choice of an appropriate number of clusters and how well
data are clustered is subjective and influenced by various factors,
including the distance metric used.

Some of the prominent stopping rules or indices dealing with clustering
validation include Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike
Information Criterion (AIC), Silhouette Score, Davies-Bouldin Index,
Homogeneity Score, Completeness Score, V-measure, Calinski-Harabasz
Index, Duda-Hartigan's Rule, and others. It's crucial to note that while
these mathematical methods can yield an optimal result, the clusters
formed may not always align with intuitive or meaningful groupings in
practical scenarios.

In applying HAC to our dataset, we introduce a variation. Instead of the
conventional Euclidean distance, we opt for the Canberra distance
(Kocher and Savoy 2017), and instead of single linkage, we employ Ward's
method (Strauss and Maltitz 2017). As we have already mentioned, we can
employ also the cosine distance (Maimon and Rokach 2010). For assessing
the optimal number of clusters we implement the Hartigan index
(Purwitasari et al. 2011) which calculates the maximum difference
between hierarchy levels of the index.

## K-means clustering

K-means clustering is a partitioning method that segments a dataset into
a predetermined number of clusters, 'k.' The data set is divided into
partitions, where each data point belongs to exactly one group. The
objective is to partition the dataset into k clusters to maximize the
data similarity within each cluster and minimize the data similarity
between clusters. It is a play with the concepts of between and within.
The process begins by specifying 'k,' although finding the optimal 'k'
can be challenging, and methods like the Elbow or Silhouette technique
are often employed (Saputra, Saputra, and Oswari 2020). The algorithm
randomly selects 'k' initial centroids (cluster centers), either from
the data points or random locations. Each point is then assigned to its
nearest centroid based on Euclidean distance (or other distance
metrics). Centroids are recalculated as the mean of all points in the
cluster, and points are reassigned to the closest new centroids. This
process iterates until points no longer switch clusters or a maximum
number of iterations is reached (typically 10 in R). The goal is to
minimize within-cluster variance, making k-means suitable for spherical
clusters; it may face challenges with clusters of varying densities or
non-spherical shapes.

Let us apply the k-means algorithm to our example dataset, consisting of
six words and two documents. Because k-means involves a degree of
randomness in its initial cluster assignments, this can lead to
different outcomes in separate runs. The randomness is managed in R
using the `kmeans()` function's `nstart` parameter, which attempts
multiple initial configurations (25 in our case) to find the best
starting point. This approach helps mitigate the algorithm's tendency to
converge to local optima. To make the algorithm's process more
understandable, we will use the `animation` library in R to create a
visual clustering sequence.

For our example, we will form the data into a table (`dt` variable) and
choose to segment it into two clusters, showcasing how k-means
partitions the data without the hierarchical structure seen in previous
methods like HAC. The version printed on paper is not that spectacular,
you will see only the final result, but once one runs the code on their
machine, one can appreciate and understand the power of animation as an
educational tool.

that the algorithm may perform poorly when cluster sizes and densities

skew the clustering results since k-means is sensitive to extreme

in shape, meaning it struggles with non-spherical (e.g., elongated or
oblong) clusters. In a two-dimensional space, k-means is most effective
when data points within a cluster are equidistant from the cluster
center, forming a circular pattern. However, this is only sometimes the

The k-means algorithm has several variations tailored to different data
characteristics. One such variation is k-medians, where the cluster's
center is determined by the median rather than the mean, offering
robustness against outliers (Arora, Raghavan, and Rao 1998). Another
variation, k-medoids, designates an actual data point as the cluster
center, usually the most centrally located point in the cluster,
enhancing the interpretability of the clusters (Rdusseeun and Kaufman
1987). Spherical k-means is particularly useful for high-dimensional
data. In this approach, data points are considered as vectors on a unit
sphere, and the cosine similarity is used as the distance metric, making
it suitable for text and other high-dimensional data (Dhillon and Modha
2001).

### Considerations on k-clustering

K-means clustering is a widely used algorithm due to its simplicity and
computational efficiency, making it suitable for large datasets with
multiple variables. The algorithm's core requirement is specifying the
number of clusters (k), and it adapts to new cluster partitions by
simply changing this value. It performs efficiently when clusters are
spherical or circular in a 2D plane, often resulting in tightly grouped
clusters with clear interpretation compared to hierarchical clustering.

However, selecting the optimal number of clusters can be challenging,
and results may vary between runs due to the algorithm's sensitivity to
initial conditions. This inconsistency is a notable limitation.
Moreover, k-means typically assumes clusters of approximately equal
sizes, which may not align with the underlying distribution of the data.
Another limitation is its exclusive focus on numerical data, which
relies on calculating Euclidean (or other types of) distances between
points.

## Gaussian Mixtures Clusterig

Gaussian Mixture Clustering (GMC), often employed in conjunction with
the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, is a sophisticated method
for identifying underlying patterns in data (Bouman et al. 1997). This
approach assumes that data points are generated from a combination of
several Gaussian (or Normal) distributions, each representing a distinct
cluster. The term 'Mixture' in GMC reflects this concept of multiple,
potentially overlapping, Gaussian distributions within the dataset.
Unlike the 'hard clustering' approach of k-means, where each data point
is distinctly assigned to one cluster, GMC adopts a 'soft clustering'
technique. Each data point is associated with probabilities denoting its
likelihood of belonging to different clusters, allowing for more nuanced
and probabilistic classification. In GMC, each Gaussian distribution
within the model is defined by its own mean and covariance, shaping the
cluster's appearance in the data space. The EM algorithm plays an
essential role in GMC by iteratively estimating and refining these
parameters, aiming to maximize the fit of the model to the observed
data.

This model is handy in scenarios where data does not conform to the
spherical cluster assumption of k-means, as illustrated in cluster 1 of
Figure [1.10](#fig-oblongandnot){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-oblongandnot"}. For instance, GMC can effectively handle
elongated or irregularly shaped clusters. In R, the Gaussian Mixture
Model can be implemented using the `GMM()` function from the `ClusterR`
package (Mouselimis 2023). The process typically involves converting
data into a suitable format (like a data frame) and specifying the
desired number of clusters. The `predict_GMM` function assigns each data
point to clusters based on the probabilities calculated by `GMM()`. It
is advisable to explore the functions' parameters and settings to
understand their application. We will explore this type of clustering
using 3 clusters.

### Considerations on GMM

GMM introduces significant flexibility compared to k-means, particularly
in accommodating clusters of different shapes, orientations, and
variances. This is partly due to GMM's ability to model each cluster
with its own covariance structure. Unlike the hard clustering of
k-means, where each object is assigned to a single cluster, GMM allows
for probabilistic cluster assignments. This soft clustering approach is
particularly advantageous in text analytics, where words often relate to
multiple topics. In GMM, centroid updates consider both mean and
variance, capturing more complex data structures than the mean-only
updates of k-means. However, GMM assumes Gaussian distributed data and
may have difficulties with highly correlated datasets. It also requires
careful selection of the number of clusters and can be computationally
demanding, particularly for large datasets. Despite these limitations,
GMM is a powerful algorithm that performs well, especially where data
exhibits complex, overlapping clustering patterns.

## Density-based clustering

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is
ideal for datasets where clusters are not necessarily spherical,
offering, like GMC, a flexible alternative to methods such as k-means.
It operates based on two parameters: a radius $\epsilon$ and a minimum
number of points (minPts). DBSCAN forms clusters from dense regions of
data points, delineated by the $\epsilon$ radius, and identifies
outliers in low-density areas. Unlike some algorithms, it does not
require the pre-specification of the number of clusters, as these
naturally emerge from the data based on the density criteria. It is
important to know that if a data point falls within the $\epsilon$
radius of multiple clusters, it is typically assigned to the first
cluster formed. Choosing the right $\epsilon$ can be challenging, as it
significantly affects the resulting clusters, but there are methods to
find both, $\epsilon$ and minPts (Ozkok and Celik 2017).

HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications
with Noise) extends DBSCAN's capabilities by transforming its flat
clustering into a hierarchical structure. This method extracts clusters
based on their stability over varying density levels, with no need for
the user to specify $\epsilon$. It is particularly good at handling
datasets with clusters of different densities, automatically determining
the optimal scale for $\epsilon$ and revealing consistent clusters over
a range of densities.

We will use the HDBSCAN algorithm to explore density-based clustering,
available in the `dbscan` package. This approach requires setting
hyper-parameters, which often depend on the dataset and specific
clustering objectives. HDBSCAN's ability to identify stable clusters
without predefined parameters like $\epsilon$ makes it a robust and
user-friendly choice in case of complex datasets.

### (H)DBSCAN clustering considerations.

DBSCAN and its variant, HDBSCAN, separate clusters into high-density and
low-density but has difficulties when densities are similar. Unlike in
the previous clustering algorithms, the shape of the clusters can be
any, outliers are dealt well with and there is no no need to set the
number of clusters ahead. You have to tune the $\epsilon$ and the
minimum points parameters though. The order of the data has an influence
on the final result and you need sufficient data to separate dense
areas, otherwise you obtain poorer results.

As a practical example, in case you have a business and we want to
improve your products based on consumer's feedback. We can apply DBSCAN
to our data set and find clusters based on the reviews. Using the
created clusters we can find similarities between reviews, for example,
if one customer writes about the screen, memory and connection of a
tablet and another one about the screen and memory, then it is useful to
inquiry about the connection too. In a commercial setup, if one customer
talks about product 1, 2 and 3, and another one only about the first
two, a recommender system might propose the third product to the second
customer.

## Affinity propagation clustering

Affinity propagation clustering as delineated by (Frey and Dueck 2007;
Dueck and Frey 2007; Dueck 2009) introduces the concept of "exemplars".
These are the foundational elements or the core nodes of a dataset
around which clustering is performed. The idea that holds this concept
is that all the elements are potential exemplars and a message system
between data points iteratively identifies a subset of exemplars and
their respective clusters.

The implementation is done by the `apcluster` package (Bodenhofer,
Kothmeier, and Hochreiter 2011). Unlike in the previous clustering
algorithms, the data can be of any shape and variable density. Another
important thing is that, the basic version of apc will calculate the
optimal number of clusters without pre-specification. Nevertheless, the
function `apclusterK()` in the `apcluster` package provides a mechanism
to specify the cluster count a priori. In the function, the first
parameter, `s`, calculates the similarity function using the negative
squared distances by default, although alternative similarity measures,
such as Spearman correlation, can be employed to capture the
relationships within certain datasets.

The second parameter, `x`, represents the distance matrix we derived
from on our previous analysis with k-means. Despite the possibility of
setting a predetermined number of clusters via $K$, the adaptive nature
of the apcluster() function's algorithm can ascertain the optimal
cluster count.

The examplars are chosen based on message passing between data points
regarding their suitability as the most representative point of a
cluster. Imagine the reviews dataset in our book. We need to want to
group reviews that are similar between them. Each review can "send a
message" to other reviews revealing its topic. If another review has the
same topic, they group. Some reviews will receive many messages back,
showing that they are very popular within that topic. These reviews
become their groups' exemplars and have the strongest connection to all
the other reviews. The process continues until each review is grouped.
Eventually, the corpus will be organized into sections where each group
will be characterized by a topic led by an exemplar that distills the
essence of that topic.

We may wonder which clustering algorithm performs optimally or yields
the most appropriate number of clusters for our specific dataset.
Various methods and packages, like `cValid`, offer comparative analyses
of clustering algorithms by evaluating their stability and validity
across different datasets. However, the effectiveness of these
algorithms can significantly depend on the nature of the dataset,
including its size, dimensionality, distribution, and the presence of
outliers. Familiarity with these characteristics and hands-on experience
is crucial in selecting the most suitable clustering approach.
Practicing with diverse datasets and challenges is essential to

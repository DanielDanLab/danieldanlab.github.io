## Meaning, Levels and Tasks of Sentiment Analysis

Sentiment analysis is a widely used technique in text analytics. The
word "sentiment" is frequently interchanged with "emotion," and it has
developed meanings in several subjects, such as literature, visual arts,
or psychology. Psychologists and philosophers often disagree about their
meaning. The word sentiment originates from the French term "sentement",
and, as by the Oxford Dictionary, is "What one feels with regard to
something; mental attitude (of approval or disapproval, etc.); an
opinion or view as to what is right or agreeable.." In our context,
sentiment is an opinion expressed in a text, be it a review or feedback
that can be positive, negative, or neutral. Emotions may reveal the
expression of specific feelings such as happiness, joy, anger, disgust,
or surprise, and can be detected. Another name for sentiment analysis is
emotion detection in text corpora (Alslaity and Orji 2024). In this
chapter, we will analyze both sentiment and emotion. Sentiment analysis
is applied in several fields, such as finance Tirunillai and Tellis
(2012) to predict stock movements and assess economic indicators,
healthcare (Denecke and Deng 2015) to improve patient care, customer
service (K. S. Kumar, Desai, and Majumdar 2016) to improve customer
care, entertainment (Baid, Gupta, and Chaplot 2017) to understand
reactions to movies or music shows, product development (Fang and Zhan
2015) to improve product features, psychology (Xinyu Wang et al. 2013)
to analyze emotional states, politics (Bakliwal et al. 2013) to
understand opinion on policies or voter sentiments, social sciences
(Pozzi et al. 2016) to understand societal trends, or marketing Berger
et al. (2020) to analyze the valence of texts in social media posts,
consumer reviews, and other customer and firm-generated content.

In marketing, sentiment analysis can provide valuable insights into the
disaggregated and aggregated opinion about products and services over a
determined period of time on a specific topic or in comparison to
competing brands (see, e.g., T. Y. Lee and Bradlow (2011)). Based on the
language used, customers also can be classified by gender and age,
allowing for better market segmentation (see, e.g., Hartmann et al.
(2019)).

The corpus to be analyzed can be decomposed into conceptual levels. We
will use an example to understand better. Let us have a document, in our
case a short review: "I purchased this tablet for Christmas and have
mixed feelings about it. The tablet quality was fantastic, but the WiFi
connection was disappointing." The highest level is the document level,
where sentiment analysis can be performed on each document of a
collection. In our review we have only one document. If the document is
a chapter, a page or a paragraph, its polarity can be assessed as
positive or negative. The next level is the sentence level. In some
instances, it is much better to consider a document at the sentence
level because the polarities of the sentences can be aggregated (see
Büschken and Allenby (2016)). Then comes the phrase level. In the
sentence, "The tablet quality was fantastic, but the WiFi connection was
disappointing.", the first phrase, "The tablet quality was fantastic,"
has a positive sentiment, while the second one, "but the WiFi connection
was disappointing," has a negative sentiment. Phrase-based sentiment
analysis focuses on different parts of a sentence, in contrast with
sentence-based which yields an averaged sentiment for the whole
sentence. The last one is the aspect level. Sentiment analysis focuses
on specific aspects (topics) within a phrase. For example, "tablet
quality" (aspect) is associated with "fantastic," which has a positive
sentiment, while "WiFi connection" is associated with "disappointing,"
which has a negative sentiment, see
Figure [1.1](#fig-salevels){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-salevels"}.

Information can be divided into facts and opinions. While facts are
expressed objectively, opinions are often representative of people's
biases, emotions, state of mind, or interests (Liu et al. 2010) and are
subjective. A document is deemed subjective if it contains adjectives,
not all of them but a subset of them (Rittman et al. 2004). Opinion
measurement is of great importance because it can influence companies'
or other people's behavior in very unpredictable ways (Atwal, Bryson,
and Kaiser 2022). Sentiment classification is one of the main tasks for
categorizing text into positive or negative, and the most common one is
polarity detection (Cambria et al. 2015). Cross-lingual classification
uses one language as training data to classify documents in another
language (Olsson, Oard, and Hajič 2005). Cross-domain classification is
similar, it uses a sentiment classifier trained on a source domain and
used on a different domain (Bollegala, Mu, and Goulermas 2015). Both
classifications are useful to cut annotation data costs. Deception
detection and opinion spam detection are processes focused on
identifying and classifying intentionally designed fake reviews aimed to
mislead and influence readers (Rosso and Cagnina 2017).\
Sentiments can be expressed either explicitly, "slow and unresponsive
touchscreen experience," or implicitly, "I could use it for only 5
minutes". Both sentences are an expression of a negative sentiment, and
there are several techniques such as subjectivity detection, polarity
detection, and sentiment strength detection (Saad 2014) that accomplish
this task. Aspects, or topics, can be identified using LDA (see Chapter
7); there are sentiment analysis aspect models that consider the
sentiment towards a specific topic included in the topic model (Lu et
al. 2011). There are several methods to accomplish this task, such as
syntax-based, supervised, unsupervised, or hybrid machine learning
(Schouten and Frasincar 2015). For a visual representation of the
sentiment analysis tasks, see

## Sentiment Analysis Methodology

Several methods are available to perform sentiment analysis:
lexicon-based, machine learning-based, or hybrid approach Dhaoui,
Webster, and Tan (2017). An extra approach can be considered, too, that
contains transfer learning and aspect-based approaches (Wankhade, Rao,
and Kulkarni 2022). The lexicon-based approach is the most common one.
It uses a dictionary and deals with assigning scores to words. This can
be problematic, though, because, depending on the context, words can
have a positive or negative polarity, so domain detection is important
(H. Han et al. 2018). Such an algorithm takes a text, compares it
against a dictionary that has scores annotated with a specific polarity
and then aggregates them to form a final score (Taboada et al. 2011).
Given that we have studied classification (see Chapter 6), there are
several ways to accomplish text classification. What is needed is to
assign a score to a text, something that we frequently see on opinion
collections on the internet: a review connected to a score that ranges
normally between 1 and 5 stars. A hybrid method is a corpus-based
approach, which is a text classification problem that uses an annotated
data set based on a lexicon (Abdulla et al. 2013). Here, we disagree
with what is depicted in
Figure [1.3](#fig-saapproach){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig-saapproach"} in (Wankhade, Rao, and Kulkarni 2022), and
we edited their image considering our observations. Our figure presents
various methods to perform sentiment analysis, which are categorized
into several approaches. Most of them fall under the Machine Learning

## Performing Sentiment Analysis in R

In R, several packages can be used to accomplish a sentiment analysis
task. `sentiment` (T. W. Rinker 2021), `syuzhet` (Matthew L. Jockers
2015), and `SentimentAnalysis` (Proellochs and Feuerriegel 2023) use the
lexicon-based approach. They employ dictionaries of words associated to
a sentiment score. `sentimentr`, uses the Jockers Rinker Sentiment
Lexicon, based on a combination of several lexicons. It takes into
account several interesting features, such as negators, amplifiers, and
de-amplifiers, to fine-tune the sentiment scores. The result is an
intensity polarity scale between very negative and very positive
\[-1,1\], passing through a neutral 0. `syuzhet` uses scores from
different dictionaries, such as Bing (M. Hu and Liu 2004) that binarily
classifies words as positive or negative without an intensity, the NRC
emotion lexicon, Khoo and Johnkhan (2018), developed at the National
Research Council of Canada (NRC), which associates words to eight basic
emotions, such as anger, fear, anticipation, trust, surprise, joy and
disgust, and Afinn (Aung and Myo 2017), which assignes integer scores to
words, that discretely range from \[-5, 5\]. It also assigns positive or
negative sentiment to the result. `SentimentAnalysis` supports several
lexicons, such as the QDAP dictionary (T. Rinker, Rinker, and LazyData
2018), the Harvard IV dictionary (Frankel, Jennings, and Lee 2022), and
many more. `vader` (Roehrick 2020) implements the Valence Aware
Dictionary for Sentiment Reasoning (VADER, Hutto and Gilbert (2014))
which is a rule-based classifier. It uses a predefined dictionary and
considers the context of the words, being "aware" of the surrounding
words and their impact on the sentiment of the target word. Sentiment
scores are adjusted based on intensity modifiers, such as "very" or
"extremely" or punctuation and capitalization ("GREAT!!" is more intense
than "great"), and negations are dealt with by flipping the sentiment
score.

The `sentiment.ai` package (Wiseman, Nydick, and Wisner 2022) uses deep
learning to accurately perform sentiment analysis on text that is
context-dependent. It uses transformer-based models, which are
pre-trained on large data sets, to identify the context in which sarcasm
and ambiguous phrases appear. Its multi-language support is a plus.
sentiment.ai seems to identify much better the negative context than
other packages [^1] as by the authors' claims, being in disagreement
with the lexicon-based packages, while on the positive side there is
agreement between them.

### Sentiment Score and Ratings

It is extremely frequent to be asked to encode an opinion on a product
or service into a numerical value, typically a "star" value between 1
and 5, with one being the most negative and five the most positive. This
representation is a quick snapshot of the overall satisfaction given by
a customer, and it is a categorical variable (Al-Natour and Turetken
2020). One can understand that this variable is not ordinal because the
subjective opinion of each reviewer gives it, and, as such, the
"distance" between 1 star and 2 is not the same as the distance between
4 and 5. Is this reflected in the sentiment score, too? Sometimes yes,
sometimes not. The emotions expressed in words can travel on a different
path than what is given as a rating. Combining these two aspects, we
might get a more comprehensive understanding of customer feedback. Is a
sentiment score directly correlated to the rating given? Let us discover
it with a scatter plot.

In many marketing contexts, customers, when they write a review, also
encode their opinion about a product or service into a numerical value,
typically a "star" value between 1 and 5, with one being the most
negative and five the most positive. This representation is a quick
snapshot of the overall satisfaction given by a customer, and it is a
categorical variable (Al-Natour and Turetken 2020). One can understand
that this variable is not ordinal because the subjective interpretation
of each reviewer of the "distance" between 1 star and 2 is not
necessarily the same as the distance between 4 and 5. In fact, in
real-world settings, the majority of customers typically contribute
overly favorable ratings, leading on the aggregate to so-called J-shape
distributions. Such rating distributions are extremely skewed with a
mass at the positive end of the rating scale (e.g., Moe, Netzer, and
Schweidel (2017)). Similarly, the emotions expressed in words can travel
on a different path than what is given as a rating. The automatically
calculated sentiment score, tries to reveal an emotional content that
star ratings alone may not capture. Combining these two aspects, we
might get a more comprehensive understanding of customer feedback. Is a
sentiment score directly correlated to the rating given? The idea is to
compare these two metrics to see if meaningful differences emerge. Let
us discover it with a scatter plot.

Why does this happen? (Valdivia, Luzón, and Herrera 2017) observed that
users sometimes write negative sentences on positive ratings and the
opposite. Their suggestion is to avoid setting the user rate as a label
for sentiment, meaning that if a rating is 1, it does not mean it is
bad; if it is 5, it does not mean it is good. It is much better to check
the negative opinions using sentiment analysis and, after that, compare
them to the rating. However, the ratings may be influenced by other
factors, such as J-shape distributions and underreporting of those with
negative attitudes (Hydock, Chen, and Carlson 2020), asymmetric social
ratings (Muchnik, Aral, and Taylor 2013), or higher attrition rates
among customers with moderate brand experiences (Brandes, Godes, and
Mayzlin 2022). While some researchers propose avoiding strict use of
user ratings as sentiment labels, it must be considered that multiple
structural and behavioral biases could also explain why some highly
negative reviews receive high ratings (and vice versa). We will not give
an example with low sentiment.ai and high ratings, but we will let this
be an exercise for you. Hint: Modify the previous code with

### Emotions Classification

Emotion measurement relies on many hints. We may see expressions of
subjective feelings in people's posture, gestures, and facial
expressions, sometimes as a physiological response or an overt (or
covert) action. In product reviews we are typically dealing with
post-purchase behavior. It seems that "frustration, anger,
disappointment, alienation, disgust, anxiety, alarm, guilt, shame, joy,
happiness, hope, pride, jubilation, excitement, relief, amusement, and
pleasure" are reactions that customers experience in various negative or
positive circumstances (Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999).

Let us consider the five main emotions, which are categorized as joy,
sadness, anger, fear, and disgust. Each emotion can be defined by a set
of words that are, in general, associated with that feeling. The 2015
cartoon movie by Pixar, Inside Out, depicts these emotions with colors.
Nevertheless, we could potentially could replace these colors with
emojis to represent each emotion, as highlighted by the approach in
(Hotz-Behofsits, Wlömert, and Nabout 2025). Here, we link the colors in
the movie with the literature that describes these words. Joy is yellow
and is characterized by words such as happy, cheer, content, excitement,
pleasure, delight, or satisfaction (Tissari 2008). Sadness is blue,
characterized by words like sad, unhappy, distress, upset, sorrow,
grief, despair, or dismay (Wierzbicka 1992). Anger is red and
characterized by anger, fury, enragement, mad, annoyance (W.-F. Chen,
Chen, and Ku 2015). Fear is violet, it is characterized by words like
afraid, scared, frightened, petrified, horrified, terrified, dread,
alarmed, panicked, anguished, anxious, worried, concerned, and
apprehensive (Wierzbicka 1990), which is an interestingly long list.
Disgust is green and is characterized by these words: disgust, repulse,
nausea, distaste, dislike, loath, or revulse (Mizin et al. 2023). These
words may not be complete, but they are sufficient for our example of
classifying comments by emotions regarding a brand, for example,
Amazon's Kindle.

## Vulgar words in negative reviews

Profanity is referred to the use of vulgar words, considered
unnacceptable, especially in formal contexts. Obviously, these terms are
an expression of extreme emotional arousal (Stephens and Zile 2017) and,
in turn, will elicit emotions in the reader, considering the reviews
with such words more credible (Rassin and Heijden 2005). Interestingly
enough, such kind of language is positively related to honesty (Feldman
et al. 2017). This information is not an incentive to make you swear, or
swear more. If you ever review this book and leave comments, we will
appreciate your honesty anyway, so there is no need to use bad words.
There are several effects of the use of vulgar words in online reviews.
For example, one of them, is never to receive an answer back from the
management (Dan 2023) of a hotel, a product line, or a customer service.
Profanity has an effect on the credibility on the usefulness of the
review, and this has been researched (Hair and Ozcan 2018). Let us take
two brands and check from the negative reviews which brand collects more
"dirty" words.
